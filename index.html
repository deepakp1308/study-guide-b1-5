Term Definition
Particles (In this context) the smallest pieces of which a substance is made; particles can be molecules, atoms or ions
Diffusion The net movement of particles from a region of their higher concentration to a region of their lower concentration (i.e. down a concentration gradient), as a result of their random movement
Net movement Overall or average movement
Concentration gradient An imaginary 'slope' from a high concentration to a low concentration
Random movement Movement in any direction, purely by chance; particles travel in straight lines and change direction when they collide with other particles
Kinetic energy The energy of movement; the energy for diffusion and osmosis comes from the kinetic energy of the moving particles, not from respiration
Rate of diffusion How fast particles diffuse; it is faster at a higher temperature, with a larger surface area, a steeper concentration gradient and a shorter diffusion distance
Diffusion distance The distance particles have to travel, e.g. the thickness of a membrane; the shorter the distance, the faster the diffusion
Surface area to volume ratio The surface area of an object divided by its volume; cutting a cube into smaller cubes increases this ratio, so substances diffuse in faster
Independent variable The variable that you change in an experiment
Dependent variable The variable that you measure as you collect your results
Standardised variable A variable that you keep the same throughout an experiment so that it cannot affect the results (also called a control variable)
Solvent The liquid in which a substance dissolves; in the solutions in and around cells, the solvent is water
Solute A substance that is dissolved in a solvent, e.g. sugar dissolved in water
Partially permeable membrane A membrane that lets some molecules through (e.g. small water molecules) but not others (e.g. much larger sugar molecules); cell membranes are partially permeable
Osmosis The diffusion of water molecules through a partially permeable membrane
Osmosis (in terms of water potential) The net movement of water molecules from a region of higher water potential (dilute solution) to a region of lower water potential (concentrated solution) through a partially permeable membrane
Water potential A way of describing how much water a solution contains compared with dissolved solute; a dilute solution has a high water potential, a concentrated solution has a low water potential, and pure water has the highest water potential of all
High water potential An area where there are a lot of water molecules, that is, a dilute solution
Low water potential An area where there are not many water molecules, that is, a concentrated solution
Water potential gradient A difference in water potential between two areas; water moves down it, from a high to a low water potential
Turgid A description of a plant cell that is tight and firm
Turgor pressure The pressure of the water pushing outwards on a plant cell wall
Flaccid A description of a plant cell that has lost its turgor pressure and is soft
Plasmolysed A description of a cell in which the cell membrane tears away from the cell wall
Plasmolysis What happens when a plant cell loses so much water by osmosis that its cell membrane tears away from the cell wall; it can kill the cell
Wilting The drooping of a plant that happens when its cells become flaccid and the plant loses its firmness
Active transport The movement of molecules or ions through a cell membrane from a region of lower concentration to a region of higher concentration (i.e. against a concentration gradient) using energy from respiration
Carrier proteins (protein carriers) Protein molecules in cell membranes that can use energy to change shape and move ions or molecules into or out of a cell
Root hair cell A cell on the surface of a plant root that takes in water and mineral ions from the soil; it uses active transport to take in nitrate ions
Dialysis A treatment for kidney failure in which unwanted substances diffuse out of the blood, through a very thin membrane, into dialysis fluid that then flows away
Indicator A dye that changes colour depending on pH; cresol red is red-purple in alkaline conditions and turns yellow when acid is added

B1 Cells and Organisms

B1.01 Characteristics of living organisms

Before You Start

The chapter begins with two questions to discuss with a partner.

Q: The list below contains some features of living organisms: breathing, excretion, a blood system, a nervous system, sensitivity, growth, reproduction, movement, nutrition, respiration. Which of these features are found in all living organisms?

A: Excretion, sensitivity, growth, reproduction, movement, nutrition and respiration. These are the seven characteristics of living things. Breathing, a blood system and a nervous system are not found in all organisms: plants and bacteria do not breathe (breathing is not the same as respiration), and they have no blood system and no nervous system.

Q: If you have a microscope, how can you distinguish between a cell from a plant, and a cell from an animal?

A: Look for a cell wall. All plant cells have a cell wall outside the cell membrane, but animal cells never do. You could also look for chloroplasts and a large central vacuole: if a cell has chloroplasts, it is a plant cell. However, a cell without chloroplasts could still be a plant cell (for example, a root cell), so the cell wall is the most reliable clue.

What Is an Organism?

Biology is the study of organisms. An organism is a complete living thing, such as yourself, a platypus, a bacterium or a mango tree. There are very many different kinds of organism on Earth, but all of them share seven characteristics (Figure B1.01).

Some non-living things have some of these characteristics, but no non-living thing has all of them. For example, a car moves, takes in fuel and gives out waste gases, but it cannot grow or reproduce.

The Seven Characteristics of Living Organisms

A useful way to remember the seven characteristics is MRS GREN: Movement, Respiration, Sensitivity, Growth, Reproduction, Excretion, Nutrition.

Characteristic What it means Example
Movement All organisms are able to move to some extent. Most animals can move their whole body from place to place, and plants can slowly move parts of themselves A bird flying; a plant's leaves slowly turning towards the light
Respiration All organisms break down glucose and other substances inside their cells, to release energy that they can use. This energy is used to make other chemical reactions in their cells happen Every living cell respires all the time, day and night
Sensitivity All organisms pick up information about changes in their environment, and react to the changes. The changes may be in the internal environment (such as the temperature of the blood) or the external environment (such as the intensity of sunlight) You pull your hand away from something hot; a plant shoot grows towards the light
Growth All organisms begin small and get larger, by the growth of their cells and by adding new cells to their bodies. Growth is a permanent increase in size or dry mass A young kangaroo (joey) growing into an adult
Reproduction Organisms are able to make new organisms of the same species as themselves A kangaroo producing a joey; a mango tree producing seeds
Excretion All organisms produce unwanted or toxic waste products as a result of their metabolic reactions, and these must be removed from the body Breathing out carbon dioxide made by respiration; getting rid of urea in urine
Nutrition Organisms take substances from their environment and use them to provide energy or materials to make new cells A kangaroo eating plants; a plant making its own food by photosynthesis

Metabolism

The energy released by respiration is used to make the other chemical reactions in cells happen. All of these chemical reactions together, including respiration, are called metabolism. Each individual reaction is a metabolic reaction. Metabolism produces waste products, some of them toxic, which is why every organism needs to excrete.

Measuring Growth: Why Dry Mass?

Growth can be defined as a permanent increase in size or dry mass. In exam answers, use the Cambridge syllabus wording: growth is a permanent increase in size and dry mass. The word permanent matters: if you drink a glass of water you become heavier for a while, but that is not growth.

Measuring dry mass involves killing and drying the organism, then finding the mass of its body without any water. Because the organism has to be killed, this method is used more often for plants than for animals. Dry mass is a more reliable measure of growth than ordinary (fresh) mass, because the amount of water in an organism goes up and down all the time.

Science in Context B1.01: How Many Cells in a Human Body?

In some organisms, it is possible to count the number of cells in their bodies. In organisms such as humans, we can only estimate the number, because it would be impossible to count them all. Cells also differ in their volume, mass and how closely or loosely they are packed together, so you cannot simply divide body mass by the mass of one cell.

Scientists recently calculated the numbers of different cells and cell types in different organs, and added them up. The latest estimate suggests that the body of an adult human contains around 37 trillion cells, which is 37 000 000 000 000.

Q: Can you think of any advantages for larger organisms of consisting of multiple cells, rather than just one larger cell?

A: In a multicellular organism, different cells can become specialised to carry out particular functions (for example, nerve cells, muscle cells and red blood cells), so the organism can do many different jobs efficiently. Other advantages: if some cells are damaged or die, they can be replaced and the organism survives; and small cells can exchange substances such as oxygen with their surroundings quickly, whereas in one giant cell substances would take too long to reach the middle.

Activity B1.01: Matching the Characteristics of Living Things with Their Descriptions

Aim: To learn the seven characteristics of living things and what each one means, by playing a memory card game.

Group size: A group of four or five.

You will need: 14 pieces of blank card, all exactly the same.

Method:

  1. Write the seven characteristics of living things on seven of the pieces of card.
  2. Write descriptions of each of the seven characteristics on the other seven cards.
  3. Shuffle each set of cards. Place them face down in two rows of seven.
  4. One person then selects a card from each row and turns them face up. If the name and description match, this person keeps the two cards. If they do not match, they place them face down again in the same positions.
  5. Now the next person does the same.
  6. Keep taking turns until all the cards have been taken by someone. The winner is the person with most cards at the end.

Expected results: Every name card is eventually paired with its correct description. Players who remember where cards are, and who know the descriptions well, collect the most pairs.

Conclusion: By the end you should be able to name all seven characteristics and match each one to its meaning. The cards must be identical so that nobody can recognise a card from its back.

Safety: There are no special hazards in this activity.

B1.02 Cell structure

Organisms Are Made of Cells

All organisms are made of cells. Cells are very small, so large organisms contain millions of cells. Organisms made of many cells are multicellular. Some organisms are unicellular, which means that they are made of just a single cell. Bacteria and yeast are examples of single-celled organisms.

New Cells Come from Existing Cells

All cells are made from existing cells. New cells are formed when a fully grown cell divides into two. Cells never appear from non-living material. You can read about how cells divide in Chapter B12.

Microscopes

To see cells clearly, you need to use a microscope (Figure B1.02). The kind of microscope used in a school laboratory is called a light microscope, because it shines light through the piece of animal or plant you are looking at. It uses glass lenses to magnify and focus the image. A very good light microscope can magnify about 1500 times.

Part of the light microscope (Figure B1.02) What it does
Eyepiece The lens at the top that you look through
Objective lenses The lenses just above the specimen that magnify it; there are usually several, each giving a different magnification
Focusing knob Turned to move the lenses and stage closer together or further apart until the image is sharp
Stage Where the specimen is placed (usually on a glass slide)
Mirror Reflects light up through the specimen

A photograph taken using a light microscope is called a photomicrograph. Some structures are too small to see clearly with a light microscope, so biologists use an electron microscope, which can magnify far more. A picture taken with an electron microscope is an electron micrograph.

Cell Membrane

Every cell has a cell membrane around the outside. The cell membrane is a very thin layer of protein and fat. It is very important to the cell because it controls what goes in and out of it.

The cell membrane is partially permeable. This means that it will let some substances through but not others. The cell membrane separates the contents of the cell from its environment (surroundings).

It is difficult to see the cell membrane in a plant cell, because it is pressed tightly against the inside of the cell wall.

Cell Wall

All plant cells are surrounded by a cell wall made mainly of cellulose. Animal cells never have cell walls. Cellulose belongs to a group of substances called polysaccharides (Chapter B3).

Cellulose forms fibres which criss-cross over one another to form a very strong covering to the cell. Figure B1.07 is a three-dimensional photograph of a cell wall surface, showing these criss-crossing cellulose fibres. The cell wall:

  • Helps to protect and support the cell
  • Prevents the cell from bursting if it absorbs a lot of water and swells

Because of the spaces between the fibres, even very large molecules are able to go through the cellulose cell wall. The cell wall is fully permeable, so it does not control what enters the cell; that is the job of the cell membrane.

Cell walls are also found in the cells of fungi and bacteria, but these walls are not made of cellulose.

Cytoplasm

Cytoplasm is a clear jelly. It is nearly all water, and it contains many substances dissolved in it, especially proteins. Many different metabolic reactions (the chemical reactions of life) take place in the cytoplasm.

Vacuole

A vacuole is a fluid-filled space inside a cell, surrounded by its own membrane.

  • Plant cells have very large, permanent vacuoles, which contain a solution of sugars and other substances called cell sap. When the vacuole is full, it presses outwards on the rest of the cell and helps it to keep its shape.
  • Animal cells have much smaller vacuoles, called vesicles, which also contain solutions.

Nucleus

The nucleus (plural: nuclei) is where the genetic information is stored. The information is kept on the chromosomes, which are inherited from the organism's parents. The chromosomes are made of DNA. The information carried on the DNA determines the kinds of proteins that are made in the cell.

Figure B1.08 is a photomicrograph of some plant cells dividing. The nuclei have been coloured with a blue stain, and the things that look like spider legs are chromosomes. We can normally only see chromosomes when a cell is dividing.

Chloroplasts

Chloroplasts are never found in animal cells, but most of the cells in the green parts of plants have them. They contain the green colouring, or pigment, called chlorophyll. Chlorophyll absorbs energy from sunlight, and this energy is then used to make food for the plant by photosynthesis (Chapter B5).

Chloroplasts often contain starch grains, which have been made by photosynthesis. Animal cells never contain starch grains.

Figure B1.09 shows a chloroplast from a cell in a pea plant. The large blue structure inside the chloroplast is a starch grain. The black stripes inside the chloroplast are membranes where the chlorophyll is kept.

Ribosomes

Ribosomes are tiny structures found in almost all animal cells and plant cells. They are so small that we can only see them with an electron microscope.

Ribosomes are where the cell makes proteins. The instructions on the DNA molecules are used to link together long chains of amino acids in a particular sequence. You can read more about protein molecules in Chapter B4, and more about how a cell makes them in Chapter B6.

Mitochondria

All the structures above (cell membrane, cell wall, cytoplasm, vacuoles, nucleus and chloroplasts) can be seen with a good light microscope. But some smaller structures cannot be seen clearly unless we use an electron microscope. These include mitochondria (singular: mitochondrion).

Mitochondria are found in almost all plant and animal cells. They are the parts of the cell where aerobic respiration happens. This is how energy is released from glucose (Chapter B9).

Aerobic respiration is the main way in which cells get the energy that they need to stay alive. The more energy a cell needs, the more mitochondria it has. Muscle cells, for example, are packed full of mitochondria.

Figure B1.10 is an electron micrograph of a mitochondrion, magnified 20 000 times. It is a long, oval structure. The pink lines are membranes, which separate the inside of the mitochondrion from the rest of the cell; the inner membrane makes many folds across the inside.

Figure B1.11 is a drawing of a mitochondrion cut open, showing it in three dimensions. Two membranes separate the inside of the mitochondrion from the rest of the cell, and the inner membrane is folded.

Bacterial Cells

Bacteria (singular: bacterium) are unicellular organisms. Bacterial cells are rather different from the cells of animals and plants. Figure B1.12 is a diagram of a bacterium: a capsule-shaped cell with labels for the cell wall (made of peptidoglycan, not cellulose), cell membrane, cytoplasm, ribosomes, circular DNA and plasmids.

No nucleus. The most important difference between a bacterial cell and animal or plant cells is that bacteria do not have a nucleus. Bacterial cells are also known as prokaryotic cells. 'Pro' means 'before', and 'karyotic' means 'nucleus'. Prokaryotic cells appeared on Earth millions of years before cells with nuclei appeared.

Circular DNA. Instead of chromosomes inside a nucleus, bacteria have a circle of DNA, sometimes called a bacterial chromosome. The DNA has exactly the same function as in other cells: it provides instructions for making proteins.

Plasmids. Bacterial cells often have one or more smaller circles of DNA, called plasmids. Scientists can use plasmids in the genetic modification of cells and organisms.

Cell wall. Bacterial cells always have a cell wall. Unlike plant cells, this cell wall is not made of cellulose. But its function is the same as in plant cells: it helps to support the cell, and prevents it from bursting if the cell takes up a lot of water.

Cell membrane. A partially permeable cell membrane is pressed tightly against the inside of the bacterial cell wall. As in plant and animal cells, the cell membrane controls what enters and leaves the cell.

Cytoplasm and ribosomes. Bacterial cells have cytoplasm and ribosomes, with the same functions as in animal and plant cells.

Missing structures. Bacterial cells do not have mitochondria or chloroplasts.

Identifying Cell Structures in Diagrams and Micrographs

  • Cell wall: a thick outer layer, often drawn as a double line, around plant and bacterial cells. An animal cell has only a thin single line (its membrane) around it.
  • Cell membrane: a thin line around the cytoplasm. In plant cells it lies right against the inside of the wall, so it is hard to see.
  • Nucleus: a large round or oval body, usually the darkest part of a stained cell.
  • Large vacuole: a big, clear space in the middle of a plant cell that pushes the cytoplasm and nucleus out to the edges.
  • Chloroplasts: green oval bodies in the cytoplasm of plant cells.
  • Mitochondria: small, sausage-shaped bodies. In electron micrographs, look for a double membrane with a folded inner membrane that looks like stripes.
  • Ribosomes: tiny dots in the cytoplasm, seen only in electron micrographs.
  • Vesicles: small round spaces surrounded by a membrane.
  • Bacterial cell: no nucleus; a loop of DNA lies in the cytoplasm, often with small circles (plasmids).

B1.03 Specialised cells

What Does Specialised Mean?

Multicellular organisms, such as humans or plants, may contain vast numbers of cells, and not all of these cells are alike. In a human body almost all of our cells have the same features that are found in most animal cells: a cell membrane, cytoplasm and a nucleus. But most of our cells have a particular function to perform, and their structure is modified to help them carry out that function effectively. They are specialised.

The same is true in a flowering plant: all the cells have the basic characteristics of plant cells, but they have slightly different structures that relate to their specific functions.

  • Root hair cell: has a long, thin extension (the root hair), which gives a large surface area for absorbing water and mineral ions from the soil.
  • Palisade mesophyll cell: is packed with chloroplasts, so it can absorb a lot of light energy for photosynthesis.

Tissues

Often, cells that specialise in the same activity are found together. A group of cells like this is called a tissue.

An example of a tissue is the layer of cells lining your stomach. These cells make enzymes to help digest your food. Figure B1.13 shows a row of these stomach-lining cells sitting side by side on a basement membrane. Each cell has a cell membrane, cytoplasm and a nucleus, plus vacuoles containing digestive enzymes near its top. Enzymes are shown being secreted out of the cells into the stomach.

The stomach also contains other tissues. For example, there is a layer of muscle in the stomach wall, made of cells which can move. This muscle tissue makes the wall of the stomach move in and out, churning the food and mixing it up with enzymes.

Plants also have tissues. You may already have looked at some epidermis tissue from an onion bulb. Inside a leaf, a layer of cells makes up the palisade tissue. These cells are specialised to carry out photosynthesis.

Organs

A group of different tissues that carry out a function together is called an organ. The stomach is an organ, made of tissues such as the lining tissue and the muscle tissue. Other organs include the heart, the kidneys and the lungs.

In a plant, an onion bulb is an organ. A leaf is another example of a plant organ.

Organ Systems

The stomach is only one of the organs which help in the digestion (breakdown) of food. The mouth, the stomach and the intestines are all part of an organ system called the digestive system. Other examples:

  • The heart is part of the circulatory system
  • Each kidney is part of the excretory system

The way in which organisms are built up can be summarised like this:

cells → tissues → organs → organ systems → organism

B1.04 Sizes of specimens

Why We Need Magnification

Many of the structures that biologists study are very small. Cells, for example, are so small that we cannot see them without a microscope. The photographs and diagrams of cells in this chapter are all much larger than the actual cells.

We can tell someone how much bigger an image is than the actual object by giving its magnification. The magnification of an image is how many times larger it is than the real object.

The Magnification Equation

The key equation in this chapter is:

magnification = size of image ÷ size of actual object

It can be rearranged to find either of the other two quantities:

  • actual size = size of image ÷ magnification
  • size of image = actual size × magnification

A memory aid is a triangle with image size in the top half and actual size and magnification side by side in the bottom half. Cover up the quantity you want: if the two that are left are side by side, multiply them; if one is above the other, divide the top one by the bottom one.

Two Very Important Rules

  1. Make sure that all the numbers in your calculation have the same units. It is often a good idea to convert everything to millimetres (mm) before you do anything else.
  2. Magnification is always written with a multiplication sign in front of it (×), and it has no units. For example, write ×500, never 500 mm.

Millimetres and Micrometres

Some of the objects that we study in biology are so small that even millimetres are not a suitable unit for measuring them. Instead, we use micrometres. The symbol for a micrometre is µm.

  • 1 mm = 1000 µm
  • 1 µm = 1 × 10⁻⁶ m (one millionth of a metre)
  • 1 m = 10⁶ µm (one million micrometres)
Conversion What to do Example
cm to mm Multiply by 10 8 cm = 80 mm
mm to µm Multiply by 1000 2.5 mm = 2500 µm
µm to mm Divide by 1000 40 µm = 0.04 mm
m to µm Multiply by 1 000 000 0.002 m = 2000 µm

Rule of thumb: changing to a smaller unit makes the number bigger (multiply); changing to a bigger unit makes the number smaller (divide).

Typical sizes: most plant and animal cells are about 10 to 100 µm across; bacteria and mitochondria are only a few µm long. If your answer for the size of a cell comes out as 50 000 µm (5 cm), check your working.

Worked Examples

Worked Example 1: Calculating Magnification (Same Units)

A drawing of an eraser is 120 mm long. The real eraser is 40 mm long. What is the magnification of the drawing?

  1. Equation: magnification = size of image ÷ size of actual object
  2. Both lengths are already in mm, so no conversion is needed
  3. magnification = 120 ÷ 40 = 3
  4. Answer: ×3

Worked Example 2: Converting Units First

A photomicrograph shows a cheek cell that is 30 mm wide. The real cell is 60 µm wide. Calculate the magnification.

  1. The units are different, so convert the image size to µm: 30 mm × 1000 = 30 000 µm
  2. magnification = 30 000 µm ÷ 60 µm = 500
  3. Answer: ×500

Check by working in mm instead: 60 µm ÷ 1000 = 0.06 mm, and 30 ÷ 0.06 = 500. You get the same answer whichever unit you use, as long as both sizes are in the same unit.

Worked Example 3: Calculating the Actual Size (Question B1.09)

The mitochondrion in Figure B1.10 measures about 83 mm long on the page, and the magnification is ×20 000. What is its real length?

  1. Convert the image size to µm: 83 mm × 1000 = 83 000 µm
  2. actual size = size of image ÷ magnification = 83 000 µm ÷ 20 000
  3. Answer: 4.15 µm

Worked Example 4: Calculating the Image Size

A cell that is 25 µm wide is drawn at a magnification of ×2000. How wide is the drawing, in mm?

  1. size of image = actual size × magnification = 25 µm × 2000 = 50 000 µm
  2. Convert to mm: 50 000 µm ÷ 1000 = 50 mm
  3. Answer: 50 mm

Worked Example 5: Actual Size from a Diagram in cm (Practice Question 4)

A diagram of a flower is 8 cm across, and the magnification is ×4.

  1. actual size = size of image ÷ magnification = 8 cm ÷ 4 = 2 cm
  2. Convert to mm: 2 cm × 10 = 20 mm
  3. Answer: 20 mm

Worked Example 6: Magnification of a Photograph (Practice Question 5)

The jellyfish in the photograph is 89 mm across, and the real jellyfish is 50 mm across.

  1. Both sizes are in mm, so magnification = 89 ÷ 50 = 1.78
  2. Answer: ×1.8 (to 2 significant figures)

Common Mistakes in Magnification Questions

  • Mixing units: dividing a length in mm by a length in µm gives an answer that is 1000 times too big or too small. Convert first.
  • Turning the equation upside down: for an image that has been enlarged, the magnification must be bigger than 1. If you get a number smaller than 1, you have probably divided the actual size by the image size.
  • Multiplying instead of dividing: to find the actual size you divide the image size by the magnification. Multiplying 8 cm by 4 to get 32 cm is the trap in Practice Question 4.
  • Adding units to a magnification: ×500 is correct; ×500 mm is wrong.
  • Forgetting the × sign in front of the magnification.
  • Measuring in cm and calling it mm: check which marks on your ruler you are reading.

How to Measure an Image

  1. Use a ruler marked in millimetres.
  2. Measure the length the question asks for (for example, the maximum length or the diameter), in a straight line from one edge of the object to the other.
  3. Line up the zero mark of the ruler, not the end of the ruler, with the edge of the object.
  4. Record the measurement to the nearest millimetre, and write it down in your working; examiners give a mark for it.

B1 Key Formulas and Concepts

  1. Magnification: magnification = size of image ÷ size of actual object
  2. Actual size: actual size = size of image ÷ magnification
  3. Image size: size of image = actual size × magnification
  4. Same units first: convert both sizes to the same unit (usually mm or µm) before dividing
  5. Millimetres and micrometres: 1 mm = 1000 µm, so mm × 1000 = µm and µm ÷ 1000 = mm
  6. Centimetres: 1 cm = 10 mm
  7. Metres: 1 m = 1000 mm = 1 000 000 µm (10⁶ µm), and 1 µm = 1 × 10⁻⁶ m
  8. Writing magnification: always with a × sign and no units, for example ×1500
  9. Fitting objects end to end: number that fit = total length ÷ length of one object, both in the same unit (for example 1000 µm ÷ 4.15 µm ≈ 241 mitochondria in 1 mm)
  10. Microscope power: a very good light microscope magnifies about ×1500; electron microscopes magnify far more (Figure B1.10 is ×20 000)
  11. Levels of organisation: cells → tissues → organs → organ systems → organism
  12. Seven characteristics (MRS GREN): movement, respiration, sensitivity, growth, reproduction, excretion, nutrition
  13. Aerobic respiration (in mitochondria, details in Chapter B9): glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water, releasing energy
  14. Plant cell extras: plant cell = animal cell structures + cell wall + large permanent vacuole + (often) chloroplasts
  15. Bacterial cell: cell wall (not cellulose) + cell membrane + cytoplasm + ribosomes + circular DNA + (often) plasmids; no nucleus, no mitochondria, no chloroplasts

Question B1.01

A student claimed that plants show fewer of the characteristics of living things than animals. Explain why this claim is wrong.

Answer: The claim is wrong because plants, like all living organisms, show all seven characteristics of living things. Plants simply carry out some of them more slowly or less obviously than animals, which may be why the student was misled.

  • Movement: plants cannot move their whole body from place to place, but they slowly move parts of themselves; for example, many flowers open during the day and close at night.
  • Respiration: every living plant cell respires all the time, day and night, releasing energy from glucose.
  • Sensitivity: plants detect and respond to changes in their environment; for example, shoots grow towards light.
  • Growth: plants grow throughout their lives, with a permanent increase in size and dry mass.
  • Reproduction: plants make new plants of the same species, for example from seeds.
  • Excretion: plants get rid of waste products of metabolism, such as oxygen from photosynthesis and carbon dioxide from respiration.
  • Nutrition: plants make their own food by photosynthesis and take in water, carbon dioxide and mineral ions.

Question B1.02

Consider two organisms. For each, identify:

a) the characteristics of living things that the organism carries out all the time

b) the characteristics of living things that only happen at certain times.

Answer: Answers depend on the organisms you choose. This example uses a human and a mango tree.

a) Human: respiration (every cell respires all the time), excretion (carbon dioxide leaves in every breath out, and the kidneys make urine continuously) and sensitivity (the body is always detecting changes, such as in blood temperature). b) Human: growth (mainly until adulthood), reproduction (only adults, and only occasionally), nutrition (we take in food at mealtimes) and movement of the whole body (only when we choose to move). Question B1.03

Explain the difference between a unicellular organism and a multicellular organism, giving one example of each.

B2 Movement into and out of Cells

B2.01 Diffusion

Before You Start: Particles in Solids, Liquids and Gases

The particle theory helps us to explain how matter behaves. Diffusion depends on particles being able to move, so it is worth checking that you remember how particles behave in each state of matter.

Q: Here are six statements about particles. Some of them describe solids, some describe liquids and some describe gases. Which are which?

A: Gases: statements 1 and 4. Solids: statements 2 and 6. Liquids: statements 3 and 5.

Statement State of matter
1. The particles are far apart and rarely collide with each other. Gas
2. The particles vibrate on the spot. Solid
3. The particles are close to one another but can move around. Liquid
4. The particles move freely in all directions. Gas
5. The particles stay in contact and slide past one another as they move. Liquid
6. The particles are in fixed positions, close to one another. Solid

Because particles in gases and liquids can move around freely, diffusion happens in gases, solutions and mixtures of liquids. In a solid the particles only vibrate in fixed positions, so they cannot spread out.

Science in Context B2.01: Filtering Blood

We each have two kidneys. Their job is to remove unwanted substances from the blood. The kidneys produce a solution of the unwanted substances, which is removed from the body as urine. Sometimes kidneys stop working. Then the unwanted substances build up in the blood, and some of them can be quite dangerous, so another way must be found to remove them.

Figure B2.01 is a photograph of a boy in a hospital bed attached to a dialysis machine. This is how dialysis works:

  1. Blood from a vein in his arm flows into the machine.
  2. Inside the machine, a liquid called dialysis fluid flows past his blood. There is a very thin membrane between the blood and the dialysis fluid.
  3. The unwanted substances in his blood diffuse through the membrane into the dialysis fluid (from a high concentration in the blood to a low concentration in the fluid).
  4. The dialysis fluid, now carrying the unwanted substances, flows away.
  5. His cleaned blood flows back into his body.

It takes several hours to remove all the unwanted substances from his blood, and he has to do this several times a week. Dialysis is a real-life use of diffusion through a membrane.

Q: (1) If the boy could have a kidney transplant, he would not have to spend time attached to a dialysis machine. Why do you think not everyone who needs a kidney transplant can have one?

A: There are not enough donated kidneys for everyone who needs one, so there are long waiting lists. A donated kidney must also be a close match to the patient's tissues, otherwise the patient's immune system will reject it, so a suitable match may not be available. Some patients may also be too ill to survive a major operation.

Q: (2) The dialysis fluid flows constantly through the machine. Can you suggest why this works better than just having stationary fluid in the machine?

A: Flowing fluid carries the unwanted substances away as soon as they diffuse into it, and fresh fluid containing none of these substances keeps arriving. This keeps the concentration of unwanted substances in the fluid low, so a steep concentration gradient is maintained between the blood and the fluid, and diffusion out of the blood continues quickly. In stationary fluid the unwanted substances would build up until their concentration was the same as in the blood. There would then be no concentration gradient, and net diffusion would stop.

What Is Diffusion?

Everything — including living cells — is made of atoms, molecules and ions. In this chapter we call all of these particles. Particles are always moving. When they can move freely, particles tend to spread themselves out evenly. This happens with gases, solutions and mixtures of liquids, and it is called diffusion.

Definition: Diffusion is the net movement of particles from a region of their higher concentration to a region of their lower concentration (i.e. down a concentration gradient), as a result of their random movement.

The Rotten Egg Example (Figure B2.02)

Imagine a rotten egg full of very smelly hydrogen sulfide gas in one corner of a room.

  • Figure B2.02a: at the start there is a high concentration of hydrogen sulfide molecules in one corner (near the egg) and none in the rest of the room.
  • Figure B2.02b: given time, the molecules spread evenly through all the space available.
  • Soon you cannot tell where the smell first came from, because the whole room smells of rotten egg.

Random Movement and Net Movement

  • Diffusion happens because the molecules move randomly. They do not purposefully move from one place to another.
  • Each molecule just moves by chance, changing direction when it bumps into another molecule. Some molecules might even go back into the rotten egg.
  • Net movement means overall or average movement. Some molecules go from the egg into the room and a few go from the room back into the egg, but overall most of them go from the egg into the room.
  • The energy for this movement is the kinetic energy of the particles themselves. Cells do not have to supply any energy from respiration for diffusion.

Three Conditions for Diffusion

The definition tells us that diffusion needs: (1) particles that can move, (2) a concentration gradient, and (3) random movement of the particles. Question B2.01 asks you to explain why each one is needed.

Concentration Gradients (Figure B2.03)

A place where a lot of molecules are close together has a high concentration of molecules. In the example, the rotten egg had the highest concentration of hydrogen sulfide molecules and the rest of the room had a low concentration.

Figure B2.03 uses a slope to picture this. Lots of particles are crowded at the top of the slope (high concentration of particles), there are only a few at the bottom (low concentration of particles), and an arrow points down the slope. We can imagine particles moving 'down the slope', from where there is a high concentration to where there is a low concentration. Another word for 'slope' is 'gradient', so we call this a concentration gradient. The overall result of diffusion is that particles move 'down' their concentration gradient.

Activity B2.01: Modelling Diffusion

Aim: To model diffusion, using people to represent the particles of a gas.

Diffusion Into and Out of Cells

  • When substances diffuse into or out of a cell, they must pass through the cell membrane (see Question B2.02).
  • Oxygen and carbon dioxide enter and leave cells by diffusion through the cell membrane, down a concentration gradient. For example, a respiring cell uses up oxygen, so there is less oxygen inside the cell than outside and oxygen diffuses in. The same cell makes carbon dioxide, so there is more carbon dioxide inside than outside and carbon dioxide diffuses out.

Factors Affecting the Rate of Diffusion

Diffusion can happen at different rates (speeds). How fast particles move from an area of high concentration to an area of low concentration depends on four factors:

Factor What makes diffusion faster Why
Temperature A higher temperature Particles have more kinetic energy, so their random movement is quicker
Surface area A larger surface area There is a greater area over which particles can move from one place to another
Concentration gradient A steeper gradient (a large difference in concentration between two areas) Particles move randomly towards the area of low concentration, and fewer move randomly in the opposite direction, so the net movement is greater
Diffusion distance A shorter distance (e.g. a thin membrane) Particles have less distance to travel

It is possible to investigate the effect of all four factors on the rate of diffusion in the laboratory, as the next two Experimental Skills boxes show.

B2.02 Osmosis

Water Inside and Outside Cells

Every cell in an organism's body has water inside it and outside it. Many different substances are dissolved in this water, and their concentrations may be different inside and outside the cell. This creates concentration gradients, down which water and solutes will diffuse, if they are able to pass through the membrane.

Remember: in a solution, the solvent is the liquid that does the dissolving (here, water) and the solute is the substance dissolved in it (for example, sugar).

How Osmosis Happens (Figure B2.04)

It is easiest to understand osmosis with just one solute. Figure B2.04 shows a dilute sugar solution on the left separated from a concentrated sugar solution on the right by a membrane with very small holes, or pores, in it. The figure shows:

  • Many small water molecules on both sides, with more of them on the left.
  • Large sugar molecules: only a few on the left (dilute) and many on the right (concentrated).
  • Arrows showing water molecules passing through the pores in both directions, but more often from left to right.
  • The caption reminds you that it is important to think about the diffusion of each kind of molecule separately.

Step by step:

  1. Water molecules are very small. Each one is made of two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom. Sugar molecules are many times larger.
  2. The holes in the membrane are big enough to let water molecules through, but not sugar molecules. A membrane like this is partially permeable, because it lets some molecules through but not others.
  3. There is a concentration gradient for sugar (higher on the right). Without the membrane, sugar molecules would diffuse into the dilute solution until they were evenly spread, but they cannot, because the holes are too small for them.
  4. There is also a concentration gradient for water. On the left there is a high concentration of water molecules. On the right the concentration of water molecules is lower, because a lot of the space is taken up by sugar molecules.
  5. Because there are more water molecules on the left, at any one moment more of them 'hit' a hole in the membrane and move through to the right than go the other way (right to left).
  6. Over time there is an overall, or net, movement of water from left to right. This is osmosis.

Osmosis is really just a special kind of diffusion. It is the diffusion of water molecules in a situation where the water molecules, but not the solute molecules, can pass through a membrane.

Definition: Osmosis is the diffusion of water molecules through a partially permeable membrane.

Water Potential (Figure B2.05)

It can be confusing to talk about the 'concentration' of water molecules, because the word concentration normally means the concentration of the solute dissolved in the water. It is much better to use a different term, water potential:

  • A dilute solution (where there is a lot of water) has a high water potential.
  • A concentrated solution (where there is less water) has a low water potential.
  • Pure water has the highest water potential of all.
  • A difference in water potential between two areas is a water potential gradient. Water molecules diffuse down this gradient, from a high water potential to a low water potential.

Figure B2.05 pictures a water potential gradient as a slope. On the left, at the top of the slope, there are lots of water molecules (high water potential). A partially permeable membrane crosses the slope, and on the right, at the bottom, there are only a few water molecules (low water potential). Water molecules tend to move down the slope, from high to low water potential.

Definition (in terms of water potential): Osmosis is the net movement of water molecules from a region of higher water potential (dilute solution) to a region of lower water potential (concentrated solution) through a partially permeable membrane.

Feature Dilute solution Concentrated solution
Amount of solute Little A lot
Amount of water A lot Less
Water potential High Low
In osmosis, water moves... Out of it, towards the concentrated solution Into it, from the dilute solution

Water potential and osmosis are very important because they affect how and when organisms take up water. The uptake of water by plants is covered in Chapter B7, and the uptake of water by humans in Chapter B6.

Osmosis and Animal Cells (Figures B2.06 and B2.07)

Osmosis happens whenever two solutions (or a solution and pure water) are separated by a partially permeable membrane. Cell membranes are partially permeable: they let water through easily, but other molecules and ions often cannot get through. This means osmosis happens across cell membranes.

Animal Cell in Pure Water (Figure B2.06)

The cytoplasm inside the cell is a fairly concentrated solution. The proteins and many other substances dissolved in it are too large to get through the cell membrane, but water molecules can get through. This is just like Figure B2.04: the pure water outside is the dilute solution, the cytoplasm is the concentrated solution, and the cell membrane is the partially permeable membrane. So osmosis occurs:

  1. Water molecules diffuse from the pure water (high water potential) into the cytoplasm (lower water potential), through the partially permeable cell membrane.
  2. As more and more water enters, the cell swells.
  3. The cell membrane has to stretch as the cell gets bigger.
  4. Eventually the strain is too much and the cell bursts.

Figure B2.06 shows an animal cell surrounded by pure water, with an arrow showing water entering through the cell membrane.

Animal Cell in a Concentrated Solution (Figure B2.07)

If the solution outside is more concentrated than the cytoplasm, it has a lower water potential than the cytoplasm. Water molecules diffuse out of the cell through the cell membrane. As the water leaves, the cytoplasm shrinks, so the cell gets smaller. Figure B2.07 shows a shrunken, wrinkled cell with an arrow showing water leaving.

Note: if the solution outside has the same water potential as the cytoplasm, water enters and leaves at the same rate, so there is no net movement and the cell stays the same size.

Osmosis and Plant Cells in Pure Water (Figure B2.08)

Plant cells do not burst in pure water. Plant cells are surrounded by a cell wall, which is fully permeable: it lets all molecules go through it. Although it is not easy to see, a plant cell also has a cell membrane just inside the wall, like an animal cell, and this membrane is partially permeable.

  1. A plant cell in pure water takes in water by osmosis through its partially permeable cell membrane, in the same way as an animal cell.
  2. As the water goes in, the cytoplasm and vacuole swell.
  3. The plant cell has a very strong cell wall, much stronger than the cell membrane. The cytoplasm presses out against the cell wall, but the wall resists and presses back on the contents.
  4. This stops the plant cell from bursting.

Figure B2.08 shows a plant cell in pure water, with the cytoplasm and large vacuole pushed right up against the cell wall and an arrow showing water entering through the cell membrane.

A plant cell in this state is rather like a blown-up tyre — tight and firm. It is said to be turgid. The pressure of the water pushing outwards on the cell wall is called turgor pressure.

Why turgor matters:

  • In a plant leaf, cells with plenty of water are all turgid. The pressure of the water pushing outwards on the cells' walls keeps the whole group of cells strong and firm, which helps to support the leaves.
  • Turgor pressure helps a plant that has no wood in it to stay upright.
  • Plant cells are usually turgid.

Plant Cells in a Concentrated Solution (Figures B2.09 and B2.10)

Now imagine a plant cell placed in a solution that is more concentrated than its cytoplasm:

  1. Water leaves the cell by osmosis, out of the cytoplasm and vacuole, through the partially permeable cell membrane.
  2. The cytoplasm and vacuole shrink.
  3. The cell wall is strong and stays in position, so the cytoplasm pulls away from the cell wall, pulling the cell membrane away from the wall too.

Flaccid: when a plant cell loses a lot of water, it also loses its turgor pressure. The contents no longer push outwards on the cell wall, so the cell becomes soft and floppy. If the cells in a plant become flaccid, the plant loses its firmness and begins to wilt.

Plasmolysed: when the cell membrane tears away from the cell wall, the cell is said to be plasmolysed. Plasmolysis can kill a plant cell, because the cell membrane is damaged as it tears away from the cell wall.

Figure B2.09 is a diagram of a plant cell in a concentrated solution. The labels show the concentrated solution outside the cell, the less concentrated solution inside the cell, the cell wall (still the same size and shape) and the cell membrane, which has pulled away from the wall. The gap between the cell wall and the shrunken cell membrane is labelled space X.

Figure B2.10 is a photograph of onion cells that have been placed in a concentrated solution. The cytoplasm has lost so much water, and shrunk so much, that it has pulled away from the cell wall. The labels point to the cytoplasm, the cell wall, the cell membrane that has pulled away from the cell wall, and the space between the cell membrane and the cell wall.

B2.03 Active Transport

Why Cells Need Active Transport

Cells often need to take in substances that are only present in small quantities around them. If a substance has a lower concentration outside the cell than inside, we would expect it to diffuse out of the cell. If the cell needs this substance, it has to do something to make it go into the cell instead of diffusing out.

What Is Active Transport?

Cells can use energy to make substances move across their membranes up the concentration gradient (from a low concentration to a high concentration). This process is called active transport.

  • The energy needed is provided by respiration.
  • Aerobic respiration, in the mitochondria in the cell, releases energy for the cell to use. Some of this energy is used for active transport, moving substances against their concentration gradients.
  • This means anything that slows down respiration (such as a shortage of oxygen) also slows down active transport.

Definition: Active transport is the movement of molecules or ions through a cell membrane from a region of lower concentration to a region of higher concentration (i.e. against a concentration gradient) using energy from respiration.

Example: Root Hair Cells Taking In Nitrate Ions

  • Root hair cells in plant roots take in nitrate ions from the soil (plants use nitrate to make proteins).
  • The concentration of nitrate ions inside a root hair cell is usually higher than in the soil.
  • So the diffusion gradient for nitrate ions is out of the root hair and into the soil.
  • But root hair cells can still take nitrate ions in, using active transport.

How Carrier Proteins Work (Figure B2.12)

There are special carrier proteins in the cell membrane of root hair cells. These proteins pick up nitrate ions from outside the cell and then change shape, pushing the ions through the cell membrane and into the cytoplasm. Figure B2.12 shows a carrier protein spanning the cell membrane, with 'outside the cell' above it and 'inside the cell' below it, in three stages:

  1. The nitrate ion enters the carrier protein (from outside the cell).
  2. The carrier protein changes shape. The energy needed for it to do this is provided by respiration in the cell.
  3. The change of shape of the carrier protein pushes the nitrate ion into the cell.

The energy for the change of shape comes from the mitochondria, which carry out aerobic respiration.

B2 Key Formulas and Concepts

  1. Diffusion: higher concentration → lower concentration (down a concentration gradient) by random movement; the energy is the particles' kinetic energy, not respiration
  2. Rate of diffusion is faster with a higher temperature, a larger surface area, a steeper concentration gradient and a shorter diffusion distance
  3. Volume of a cube = side × side × side; surface area of a cube = 6 × side × side
  4. One 1 cm cube: volume 1 cm³, surface area 6 cm², ratio 6 : 1; eight 0.5 cm cubes: total volume 1 cm³, total surface area 12 cm², ratio 12 : 1
  5. Osmosis: higher water potential (dilute solution) → lower water potential (concentrated solution), through a partially permeable membrane
  6. Dilute solution = a lot of water = high water potential; concentrated solution = less water = low water potential; pure water has the highest water potential
  7. Animal cell: in pure water it swells and bursts; in a concentrated solution it shrinks
  8. Plant cell: in pure water it becomes turgid (turgor pressure; the cell wall stops it bursting); in a concentrated solution it becomes flaccid and may become plasmolysed
  9. Active transport: lower concentration → higher concentration (against a concentration gradient), through carrier proteins in the cell membrane, using energy from respiration
  10. Change in length = final length − starting length (positive = longer, negative = shorter)
  11. Percentage change = (change ÷ starting value) × 100%

B2 Textbook Questions with Answers

Question B2.01

Look at the definition of diffusion in the 'Key words' box at the start of this section. Explain why the following conditions are required for diffusion to occur:

a) movement of particles

b) a concentration gradient

c) random movement.

Answer:

a) Diffusion is the net movement of particles from one place to another, so the particles must be able to move. Their movement comes from their kinetic energy. If the particles could not move (for example, if they were held in fixed positions as in a solid), they could never spread out from a region of high concentration, so diffusion could not happen.

b) Diffusion is net movement from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration, so there must be a difference in concentration. If the concentration were the same everywhere, the particles would still move about, but just as many would move one way as the other, so there would be no net movement and no diffusion.

c) The particles do not move purposefully towards the low concentration; they move randomly in all directions, changing direction when they collide. Because there are more particles in the region of high concentration, by chance more of them move out of that region than move into it. This random movement is what produces the net movement down the concentration gradient, until the particles are evenly spread out.

Question B2.02

When substances diffuse into and out of cells, which part of the cell must they move through?

Answer: They must move through the cell membrane. The cell membrane surrounds every cell, so any substance entering or leaving the cell has to cross it. (In a plant cell, substances also pass through the cell wall, but the cell wall is fully permeable, so it is the cell membrane that controls what enters and leaves.)

Answer: When an animal cell is placed in pure water, water enters (moves into) the cell by osmosis through the partially permeable cell membrane.

Question B2.08

Animal cells burst if they are placed in pure water, but plant cells do not. Explain why.

Answer: Both kinds of cell take in water by osmosis in pure water, because the pure water has a higher water potential than the cytoplasm and the cell membrane is partially permeable. As water enters, the cell contents swell. An animal cell is surrounded only by its thin cell membrane, which stretches as the cell swells until the strain is too much and the cell bursts. A plant cell has a very strong cell wall outside its cell membrane. As the cytoplasm and vacuole swell, they press outwards on the cell wall, but the wall resists and presses back on the contents (turgor pressure). This stops the cell expanding any further, so the plant cell becomes turgid instead of bursting.

Answer: The correct order is:

  1. Water moves out of the cell through the partially permeable cell membrane.
  2. The cytoplasm and vacuole shrink.

Water leaving the cell is what makes the cytoplasm and vacuole shrink, and because the strong cell wall stays in position, the shrinking contents pull the cell membrane away from the wall.

Question B2.10

Look at Figure B2.09, which shows a plant cell in a concentrated solution: the cytoplasm and vacuole have shrunk and the cell membrane has pulled away from the cell wall, leaving a gap labelled space X between the cell wall and the cell membrane. What fills space X? Explain your answer.

Answer: Space X is filled with the concentrated solution from outside the cell. The cell wall is fully permeable, so the outside solution (water and its dissolved solute) passes straight through it. When the cytoplasm and vacuole shrink and the cell membrane pulls away from the wall, the outside solution moves through the wall and fills the gap between the cell wall and the cell membrane. It is not cytoplasm, because the cytoplasm stays inside the cell membrane, and it is not air.

Question B2.11

A group of plant cells has been placed in a concentrated solution, and the cells are plasmolysed. Predict what will happen if the cells are now placed in pure water. Explain your answer, using the term water potential.

Answer: The cells will take up water by osmosis and become turgid again (as long as they have not been killed). Pure water has a higher water potential than the cell contents, because the cytoplasm and the solution in the vacuole are concentrated solutions with a low water potential. So water moves into the cells down the water potential gradient, through the partially permeable cell membranes. The cytoplasm and vacuole swell, the cell membrane is pushed back against the cell wall, and turgor pressure builds up, so the cells become turgid. However, if a cell membrane was badly damaged when it tore away from the cell wall, that cell may have died; its membrane would no longer be partially permeable, so it would not become turgid again.

Science in Context: How Did Life on Earth Start?

One of the greatest mysteries of life on Earth is how it began. Living cells are made up of biological molecules, and these are not often found in non-living things. Scientists think that these molecules may have formed from simpler substances when the Earth was still very young, perhaps 4 billion years ago. The mixture of chemicals in the seas and atmosphere at that time, together with the energy provided by volcanic eruptions and lightning strikes, could possibly explain how the first molecules of life were produced.

Another possibility is that at least some of these molecules arrived on Earth from outer space. Meteorites are rocks that arrive on Earth from space, and they may have delivered molecules such as amino acids (the building blocks of proteins). If we can understand how life first arose from biological molecules, this could help us to answer another important question: is there life anywhere else in the universe?

Q: Discussion question 1: Most scientists think that all life on Earth today comes from other life. Suggest why scientists think it is unlikely that life could arise from non-living things today.

A: Conditions on Earth today are very different from those about 4 billion years ago. The atmosphere now contains a lot of oxygen, which reacts with and breaks down simple carbon compounds, and the mixture of chemicals in the seas and air has changed. The Earth is also full of living organisms, especially microorganisms such as bacteria, which would quickly use any new biological molecules as food or break them down before they could build up and join together. Finally, getting from simple molecules to even the simplest living cell probably took millions of years, so we would not expect to see it happen now.

Colour after heating with Benedict's solution Amount of reducing sugar
Blue (stays the same colour) None, so the result is negative
Green Very low concentration
Yellow Slightly higher concentration
Orange-red (often called brick red) Very high concentration

However, even low concentrations of glucose will eventually turn the blue colour to orange-red if they are heated for long enough. So, to compare foods fairly, heat them all for the same length of time, or time how long each one takes to change colour.

Figure B3.04 shows positive results of the Benedict's test. The tube on the left contains a small amount of reducing sugar, and the blue Benedict's solution has changed to green. The tube on the right contains a large amount of reducing sugar, and the mixture has turned orange-red.

Benedict's test: blue → green → yellow → orange-red as the amount of reducing sugar increases.

Good to know: not every sugar is a reducing sugar. Ordinary table sugar (sucrose) is not a reducing sugar, so it leaves Benedict's solution blue. This is why the book says that most sugars, rather than all sugars, can be detected with Benedict's solution.

Using a Water-Bath

The Benedict's test needs heat, and the safest way to heat the tubes is to stand them in a water-bath:

  • Electric water-bath: can be set to a temperature above room temperature (about 80 °C for the Benedict's test)
  • Home-made water-bath: heat water in a beaker over a burner, and stand the test-tube in the hot water (Figure B3.05)
  • 0 °C water-bath: fill a beaker with crushed ice
  • Keeping the temperature steady: check the temperature with a thermometer, and either heat with the burner or add cold water to keep the temperature fairly constant

Figure B3.05 shows a boiling tube containing a mixture of Benedict's solution and food, standing in a beaker of water that is being heated over a burner. A water-bath heats the mixture gently and evenly; heating a tube directly in a flame could make the liquid boil suddenly and spit out.

Method: Testing for Reducing Sugars

  1. Read through the instructions and construct a results chart.
  2. Take some of your first food and cut it into very small pieces. Put the pieces into a boiling tube and add a little water. Shake the tube to try to dissolve any sugar in the food.

  6. Repeat with several other samples of different foods.

Activity B3.01: Comparing Reducing Sugar Content

When you tested foods for reducing sugar, you probably noticed that the red colour took a few minutes to appear. The less time it takes for the red colour to appear, the more reducing sugar there is in the food. In this activity (in a group of two or three), you discuss how to use this fact to compare the quantity of reducing sugar in three different foods, and write a list of instructions that someone else could follow.

A model set of instructions:

  1. Choose three foods, for example grapes, apple and onion. Weigh out the same mass of each food (for example 2 g) and cut each one into equally small pieces.
  2. Put each food into its own labelled boiling tube, add the same volume of water (for example 5 cm³) to each, and shake each tube for the same length of time.
  3. Add the same volume of Benedict's solution (for example 2 cm³) to each tube and shake to mix.
  4. Stand all three tubes in the same water-bath at about 80 °C at the same moment, and start a stopwatch.
  5. Record the time taken for each mixture to turn red. If a tube has not turned red after 10 minutes, record "more than 10 minutes".
  6. Repeat the whole experiment twice more and calculate a mean time for each food.
  7. The food with the shortest mean time contains the most reducing sugar, and the food with the longest time contains the least.

Variables to mention in your instructions:

  • Independent variable (what you change): the type of food
  • Dependent variable (what you measure): the time taken for the red colour to appear
  • Controlled variables (what you keep the same): mass of food, size of the pieces, volume of water, volume of Benedict's solution and the temperature of the water-bath

Peer assessment: swap your instructions with another group. Check whether their instructions are easy to follow and whether they would correctly show which food contains the most reducing sugar. Fats and Oils (Lipids)

Fats and oils are also known as lipids:

  • A fat is a lipid that is solid at room temperature (for example, butter)
  • An oil is a lipid that is liquid at room temperature (for example, olive oil)
  • Lipids are insoluble in water

Fats and oils contain the same three elements as carbohydrates: carbon, hydrogen and oxygen. However, in fats each molecule contains much less oxygen than is found in a carbohydrate molecule.

Fat molecules are made of two kinds of smaller molecule: glycerol and fatty acids. Figure B3.06 shows a glycerol molecule drawn as a short upright bar and a fatty acid molecule drawn as a long zigzag line. In the fat molecule, three fatty acid molecules are joined to one glycerol molecule.

1 glycerol + 3 fatty acids → 1 fat (lipid) molecule

Why Organisms Need Fats

  • Cell membranes: fats are important for making cell membranes
  • Energy stores: fats are used as energy stores, and fat stores contain more energy per gram than carbohydrate stores
  • Insulation: mammals often have a layer of cells containing fat droplets beneath the skin. This fat-containing tissue is both an energy store and a heat insulating layer

Figure B3.08 shows a bearded seal. Bearded seals have a really thick layer of fat underneath their skin, which helps them to survive in icy Arctic waters.

Testing for Fats and Oils: The Ethanol Emulsion Test

Fats and oils do not dissolve in water, but they do dissolve in ethanol. We can use this to detect them in food:

  1. The food is cut into small pieces and shaken with ethanol. Any fats in the food dissolve in the ethanol.
  2. The ethanol is poured into a clean tube containing water.
  3. If there are fats in the ethanol, they form tiny droplets in the water, which give it a milky appearance (Figure B3.07).

The mixture of tiny droplets in water is called an emulsion, so this test is called the ethanol emulsion test. The milky colour appears because the fat cannot stay dissolved once the ethanol mixes with the water, so it comes out as millions of tiny droplets that scatter light.

Figure B3.07 shows a test-tube with a cloudy, milky white layer: this is what a positive result looks like. If there is no fat in the food, the liquid stays clear.

Ethanol emulsion test: clear → milky white emulsion means fat or oil is present.

Proteins

Protein molecules contain four elements: carbon, hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen (C, H, O and N). Some proteins, but not all, also contain a small quantity of the element sulfur.

  • Amino acids: a protein molecule is made of a long chain of smaller molecules called amino acids (Figure B3.09)
  • 20 kinds: there are 20 different amino acids
  • Precise order: each kind of protein has different amino acids linked together in a precise order (sequence)
  • One change matters: if even one amino acid is changed, or moves into a different place in the protein molecule, then we get a different protein
  • Endless variety: so there is an almost infinite number of different kinds of protein that can be made

Think of the 20 amino acids like the letters of an alphabet. By arranging letters in different orders you can make a huge number of different words; by arranging amino acids in different orders, cells make a huge number of different proteins.

Figure B3.09 shows a small part of a protein molecule as a chain of coloured blocks. Each block is one amino acid molecule, and the different colours represent different kinds of amino acid.

Many amino acids (20 kinds) joined in a specific sequence → one protein molecule

Functions of Proteins

Proteins have many different functions in organisms:

Protein Function
Enzymes All enzymes are proteins; they are biological catalysts that speed up reactions in cells (you will study enzymes in the next chapter)
Antibodies Help to protect the body against pathogens; they are secreted by white blood cells, bind to pathogens and help to destroy them
Haemoglobin The red pigment that transports oxygen in mammalian blood
Proteins in cell membranes Proteins are important for forming cell membranes in all organisms
Keratin The protein that makes up hair and fingernails in humans

Testing for Proteins: The Biuret Test

  • Biuret reagent (also called biuret solution) is blue
  • Positive result: it changes to violet (purple) when it is in contact with proteins; a pale lilac colour means only a little protein
  • Negative result: it stays blue if there is no protein
  • No heating is needed: just add the biuret solution to the food and shake

Figure B3.10 shows negative and positive results of the biuret test. The tube on the left has no protein, so it is still blue; the tube on the right contains protein, so it has turned violet.

Biuret test: blue → violet (purple) means protein is present.

Good to know: biuret reagent is a mixture of dilute copper sulfate solution and sodium hydroxide solution, and some exam questions describe adding these two solutions separately. Whichever way it is described, in an exam always say that the biuret test is the test for protein.

B3 Textbook Questions with Answers

Question B3.01

Name one carbohydrate that is found in both plants and animals.

Answer: Glucose. Plants make glucose in photosynthesis, and both plants and animals use glucose in respiration to release energy; in humans it is the form in which carbohydrate is transported in the blood plasma. (Starch is found only in plants and glycogen only in animals, so neither of these is a correct answer.)

Question B3.02

Animals that live in very cold places often have especially thick layers of fat underneath the skin (Figure B3.08). Suggest why.

(Figure B3.08: Bearded seals have a really thick layer of fat underneath their skin.)

Answer: The thick layer of fat under the skin acts as a heat insulating layer. Fat is a poor conductor of heat, so it reduces the amount of heat lost from the animal's warm body to its very cold surroundings, helping it to keep its body temperature high and constant. The fat is also an energy store: when food is scarce, the animal can use it in respiration to release energy, including heat to keep warm.

Question B3.03

Fat stores contain more energy per gram than carbohydrate stores. Suggest why birds that are about to go on long migrations build up stores of fat before they fly.

Answer: Flying a long distance needs a lot of energy, which is released by respiration in the bird's flight muscles, and the bird may not be able to feed for long periods during the journey, so it must carry its fuel with it. Fat stores contain more energy per gram than carbohydrate stores (roughly twice as much), so storing fuel as fat gives the bird the most energy for the least extra body mass. A lighter fuel store means the bird uses less energy carrying it, so it can fly further before it needs to stop and feed.

Question B3.04

Which element is found in all proteins, but not in carbohydrates and fats?

Answer: Nitrogen (N). All proteins are made of amino acids, which contain carbon, hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen, but carbohydrates and fats contain only carbon, hydrogen and oxygen. (Sulfur is found in some proteins, but not in all of them, so it is not the answer.)

Before You Start: What Happens in a Chemical Reaction?

Enzymes control chemical reactions, so the chapter begins by checking what you already know about reactions. The book gives five statements and says that two of them are wrong.

Q: Here are some statements about chemical reactions. Two of them are incorrect. Can you identify these incorrect statements? When you think you have identified them, change them so that they are correct. In a chemical reaction: one or more substances are changed to one or more new substances; a large molecule may be split into smaller molecules; atoms of one element are rearranged to make atoms of other elements; small molecules may combine to form larger molecules; products are changed to reactants.

A: The two incorrect statements are:

  • "Atoms of one element are rearranged to make atoms of other elements." This is wrong, because atoms are never changed into atoms of a different element in a chemical reaction. Corrected version: in a chemical reaction, atoms are rearranged to make new substances, but each atom stays as the same element.
  • "Products are changed to reactants." This is the wrong way round. Corrected version: reactants are changed to products.

The other three statements are correct, and enzymes catalyse exactly these kinds of reactions: splitting large molecules into small ones (for example, starch into maltose) and joining small molecules into large ones (for example, amino acids into proteins).

Science in Context B4.01: The Dangers of Dairy Products

All mammals feed their young on milk (Figure B4.01 shows young guanacos feeding on milk from their mother). Milk is an excellent food. It contains carbohydrates, fats, proteins, minerals and vitamins.

Human babies are mammals too, so they feed on milk. To help them digest the carbohydrate in milk, they produce an enzyme called lactase. Lactase breaks a sugar called lactose into smaller molecules, which can then be absorbed into the blood.

Most people stop making lactase once they are old enough to eat food other than milk. Some people keep making lactase all their lives and can drink milk and eat dairy products with no problems. Everyone else is intolerant to lactose, and dairy foods make them feel ill:

  1. They do not make lactase, so lactose is not broken down in the digestive system.
  2. The lactose stays inside the alimentary canal (the tube that food passes through).
  3. This makes water move into the alimentary canal by osmosis, and the extra water can cause diarrhoea.
  4. Bacteria living in the alimentary canal feed on the lactose and produce gases as a waste product, which can cause abdominal pain and flatulence.

Solutions to lactose intolerance: the simplest solution is not to eat foods containing lactose. Another is to swallow a pill containing the enzyme lactase before eating dairy products, which may prevent the unpleasant symptoms.

Q: Discussion question 1 — If most people stop making lactase when they are adults, there must be some advantage in this. Discuss what this might be.

A: Possible ideas to discuss:

  • Making an enzyme uses energy and raw materials (amino acids are needed to build the protein). An adult who no longer drinks milk does not need lactase, so stopping making it saves energy and amino acids for making other, more useful proteins.
  • Long ago, before people kept animals for milk, adults had no milk to drink, so lactase was of no use after a child had stopped feeding on its mother's milk.
  • Losing lactase may help a young mammal to stop feeding on its mother's milk (weaning), so the mother can save her energy and resources for her next baby.

What Is a Catalyst?

Many chemical reactions can be sped up by substances called catalysts. A catalyst alters the rate of a chemical reaction without being changed itself. Because it is not used up, a catalyst can be used again and again.

Enzymes as Biological Catalysts

Within any living organism, chemical reactions are taking place all the time. These are called metabolic reactions. Almost every metabolic reaction is controlled by catalysts called enzymes.

  • Enzymes are proteins (so they are made of amino acids).
  • Without enzymes, metabolic reactions would take place very slowly or not at all.
  • Enzymes make sure that the rates of metabolic reactions are fast enough to sustain life.

Examples of Enzymes at Work

Digestion. Inside the intestines, large molecules are broken down to smaller ones in the process of digestion. These reactions are sped up by enzymes, and a different enzyme is needed for each kind of nutrient:

  • starch → maltose (catalysed by amylase)
  • protein → amino acids (catalysed by protease)

Plants. These enzymes are also found in plants. In germinating seeds, enzymes digest the food stores (mainly starch) to feed the growing seedling.

Catalase. Catalase works inside all the cells of living organisms. Many of the chemical reactions in cells produce hydrogen peroxide, which is a very dangerous substance and must be broken down immediately. Catalase breaks it down:

  • hydrogen peroxide → water + oxygen (catalysed by catalase)

Building up molecules. Not all enzymes help to break things down. Many enzymes help to make large molecules from small ones. For example, enzymes help to link amino acids together to make proteins inside cells.

Naming Enzymes

Enzymes are named according to the reaction that they catalyse, and their names often end in -ase:

  • Enzymes that break down carbohydrates are carbohydrases.
  • Enzymes that break down proteins are proteases.
  • Enzymes that break down fats and oils (lipids) are lipases.

Some enzymes have more specific names. The carbohydrase that breaks down starch is amylase, the one that breaks down maltose is maltase, and the one that breaks down sucrose is sucrase.

The substance that an enzyme changes is called its substrate. The substrate of amylase is starch, and the substrate of lipase is lipids.

How Enzymes Work

Each type of enzyme has molecules with a very specific shape. The enzyme molecule has a 'dent' in it called the active site. Figure B4.02 shows how an enzyme catalyses a reaction:

  1. The substrate molecule has a shape that is complementary to the active site of the enzyme (a perfect mirror-image shape).
  2. The substrate fits into the active site, where the substrate and the enzyme bind together. For this to happen, the fit has to be perfect.
  3. While the substrate is in the active site and bound to the enzyme, the enzyme changes the substrate into a new substance called the product (in Figure B4.02, the substrate is split into two product molecules).
  4. The product breaks away from the enzyme.
  5. The enzyme is now free, and unchanged, so it is ready to bind with another substrate molecule.

In Figure B4.02, one substrate molecule is broken into two product molecules. Enzymes can also catalyse reactions where two substrate molecules bind with the active site and are joined together to form a single product molecule.

Enzymes are very fast. In the few minutes it takes to read about how enzymes work, a single enzyme molecule could have catalysed millions of reactions. Catalase is the fastest enzyme known: one catalase molecule can break down an almost unbelievable 44 million hydrogen peroxide molecules in one second.

The Enzyme–Substrate Complex (Supplement)

The short-lived structure that forms as the substrate slots into the enzyme's active site has its own name: the enzyme–substrate complex. The whole process can be summarised as:

  • enzyme + substrate → enzyme–substrate complex → enzyme + product(s)

The enzyme appears at both ends of this sequence, which shows that it is unchanged and can be used again.

Activity B4.01: Enzyme Specificity (Supplement)

Each enzyme can only catalyse reactions with one type of substrate. This is called enzyme specificity. The activity asks you to think about why enzymes are specific, discuss your ideas with a partner, and be ready to share them with the class.

Q: Why are enzymes specific?

A: The active site of an enzyme has a very specific shape. Only a substrate molecule with a complementary shape can fit into the active site and bind to it to form an enzyme–substrate complex. Molecules of other substances have different shapes, so they do not fit the active site and cannot be changed by that enzyme. This is like a key fitting a lock: only the key with the right shape will fit. For example, amylase breaks down starch, but it cannot break down proteins or fats, because their molecules do not fit its active site.

B4.02 Factors That Affect Enzymes

Optimum Temperature and Optimum pH

Enzymes act very quickly: each enzyme molecule can change many substrate molecules to products every second. But some factors affect this speed of action. Enzymes are very sensitive to temperature and pH.

Each kind of enzyme has a particular temperature and pH at which it works fastest. These are called the optimum temperature and the optimum pH for that enzyme. ("Optimum" means "best".)

Buffer Solutions

To investigate the effect of pH, you need to control the pH accurately. A buffer solution is a liquid that has a known pH and keeps that pH steady all the time, even if chemical reactions take place. You use a different buffer solution for each pH you want to test.

Experimental Skills B4.01: Investigating the Effect of pH on the Activity of Catalase

Aim

To find out how changing the pH affects the rate at which catalase breaks down hydrogen peroxide to water and oxygen.

How the Experiment Works

  • The reaction is: hydrogen peroxide → water + oxygen (catalysed by catalase).
  • Catalase is extracted from celery (or any other plant material, such as potato or leaves) by mashing it up in water, so that the catalase forms a solution in the water. It is best if this is done once for the whole class.
  • A small square of filter paper is soaked in the catalase solution and pushed to the bottom of a beaker of hydrogen peroxide solution. The catalase on the paper produces oxygen, the oxygen bubbles collect on the paper, and the paper floats upwards (Figure B4.05).
  • The faster the catalase works, the sooner the paper rises. So the time taken for the paper to rise is a measure of the rate of reaction: a shorter time means a faster rate.
  • Buffer solutions are used to set the pH in each beaker.

Apparatus

  • Some fresh plant material, such as celery

  • Muslin or a sieve, and a filter funnel
  • Some 3% hydrogen peroxide solution

  • 15 small squares of filter paper, all the same size (about 5 mm × 5 mm)
  • Forceps (tweezers)
  • A stopwatch or timer

Method

Before you start, practise using the forceps to pick up the little pieces of filter paper. It is not easy!

  1. Cut up some celery, put it into a blender, add water and blend. (Without a blender, cut the celery into small pieces and grind it with water using a pestle and mortar.)
  2. Pour the mixture through muslin in a filter funnel, or through a sieve, to separate most of the bits of celery from the liquid. (Filter paper would take too long.)
  3. The liquid now contains catalase. Label a beaker 'catalase solution' and pour some of the liquid into it.
  4. Copy the results table.
  5. Write the pH values of your buffer solutions into the results table, and label each small beaker with the pH of one of the buffer solutions.
  6. Measure 5 cm³ of hydrogen peroxide solution into each labelled beaker.
  7. Measure 10 cm³ of the appropriate buffer solution into each beaker.
  8. Use forceps to pick up a square of filter paper and dip it into the catalase solution (Figure B4.04).
  9. Get the stopwatch ready. Still using the forceps, push the paper to the bottom of the hydrogen peroxide solution in one small beaker, let it go, and time how long it takes to rise to the top of the liquid (Figure B4.05). Record the time.
  10. Repeat steps 8 and 9 twice more, using the same beaker, so you have three tries.
  11. Repeat steps 8, 9 and 10 for each of the other small beakers.
  12. Draw a line graph with pH on the x-axis and the mean time for the paper to rise on the y-axis. Join the points with ruled straight lines.

Answering the Experiment Questions

Q: (1) Write the word equation for the chemical reaction that took place in the small beakers.

A: hydrogen peroxide → water + oxygen (catalysed by catalase)

Q: (2) Explain, in your own words, why the pieces of filter paper rose to the top of the liquid in the beaker.

A: The filter paper was soaked in catalase. The catalase broke down the hydrogen peroxide in the beaker, producing oxygen gas. Bubbles of oxygen collected on the paper and made it buoyant (less dense than the liquid), so it floated up to the top. The faster the catalase worked, the faster the bubbles formed, and the sooner the paper rose.

Q: (3) Describe the relationship between the pH and the time taken for the filter paper to rise to the top of the liquid.

A: As the pH increases from the lowest value towards the optimum, the time taken for the paper to rise decreases. The shortest time is at the optimum pH (usually around pH 7). As the pH increases above the optimum, the time taken increases again. Quote the actual pH values and mean times from your own results table, with units (s). Remember that the shortest time shows the fastest rate of reaction.

Q: (4) Did you have any anomalous results? If so, explain why you decided that these results were anomalous. Explain how you dealt with these results when you drew your graph.

A: An anomalous result is one that does not fit the pattern: for example, one of the three tries at a pH is very different from the other two, or a mean does not fit the smooth trend of the other points. Possible causes are the paper sticking to the side of the beaker, the paper not being pushed right to the bottom, or more catalase soaking into one square than the others. To deal with it, leave the anomalous result out when calculating the mean (or repeat that try), and plot the mean of the remaining results. For example, if the three tries at one pH were 12 s, 13 s and 31 s, the 31 s result is anomalous, so the mean is (12 + 13) ÷ 2 = 12.5 s. If an anomalous point is plotted on the graph, circle it and do not join it into the line.

Q: (5) Use your results to estimate the optimum pH of the catalase that you used.

A: The optimum pH is the pH with the shortest mean time, which is the lowest point on your graph. For catalase from celery this is usually about pH 7. Because you only tested five pH values, you can only say that the true optimum is close to this value; it may lie between the pH values either side of your lowest point.

Q: (6) Suggest what you could do to obtain a more precise value for the optimum pH of this enzyme.

A: Repeat the experiment using more buffer solutions with pH values closer together around your estimated optimum (for example pH 6.0, 6.5, 7.0, 7.5 and 8.0, or even smaller steps). Do more repeats at each pH and calculate means.

Q: (7) Evaluate your experiment by identifying the main sources of error. Suggest how you could improve the experiment, to reduce these sources of error and give you more confidence that your results are correct.

A: Main sources of error and improvements:

  • Different amounts of catalase on each paper square: the squares may not be exactly the same size or may be dipped for different times. Cut the squares accurately (or use a hole punch) and dip each one for the same time, for example 5 seconds.
  • Timing by eye: reaction time when starting and stopping the stopwatch, and deciding exactly when the paper reaches the top. Use the same person and the same end point each time, or video the beakers and time from the recording.
  • The paper may stick to the side of the beaker or not be released from the very bottom. Release it in the same way, in the centre of the beaker, each time.
  • Hydrogen peroxide is used up: steps 8 to 10 reuse the same beaker three times, so the hydrogen peroxide becomes slightly less concentrated. Use fresh hydrogen peroxide and buffer for each try.
  • Temperature is not controlled: stand the beakers in a water-bath at a constant temperature, for example 25 °C.
  • The catalase solution may lose activity over time: use freshly prepared extract and keep it in the same conditions throughout.
  • Few data points: test more pH values and do more repeats, then check that the repeats are close together.

Aim and Background

To investigate how temperature affects the activity of lipase. Lipase is an enzyme that catalyses the breakdown of lipids (fats and oils). Milk contains lipids.

Denaturation

Temperature. Enzymes have an optimum temperature at which they work fastest:

  • Enzymes from the human body generally have an optimum temperature of about 37 °C.
  • Enzymes from plants may have optimum temperatures much lower than this.
  • Some bacteria, especially those that live in hot springs, have very high optimum temperatures: up to 80 °C in some cases.

For many enzymes, a temperature above about 60 °C completely stops them working. This is because the high temperature damages the enzyme. The enzyme is said to be denatured, and it cannot catalyse its reaction any more.

pH. Enzymes also have an optimum pH. For most enzymes this is around pH 7, but some enzymes work best at a much higher or lower pH. For example, in Chapter B6 you will meet an enzyme that works best in the acidic conditions of the human stomach, at a pH of about 2. When an enzyme is placed in a liquid with a pH that is not its optimum pH, it is damaged. The further the pH is from the optimum, the more the enzyme is damaged, and well away from the optimum it is denatured and cannot catalyse its reaction.

Important: low temperatures do NOT denature enzymes. At 0 °C an enzyme has almost no activity because its molecules are moving very slowly, but if it is warmed up again it will start working. Denaturation is caused by high temperatures or by a pH far from the optimum.

Explaining the Effect of Temperature on Enzyme Activity (Supplement)

Use Figure B4.03a to follow these steps:

  1. At 0 °C, there is no activity. Molecules have very little kinetic energy and move only slowly. Substrate molecules rarely collide with the enzyme, so they rarely enter its active site, and very few substrate molecules are converted to product.
  2. As temperature increases, activity increases. The kinetic energy of the enzyme and substrate molecules increases. They move faster and collide with each other more frequently and with more energy, so effective collisions are more frequent. Each second, more substrate molecules collide with an active site, form enzyme–substrate complexes and are converted to product.
  3. At the optimum (about 37–38 °C in Figure B4.03a), activity is greatest. There are the most effective collisions per second while the active site still has its correct shape.
  4. Above the optimum, activity decreases. The kinetic energy of the enzyme begins to shake it apart. Its molecules begin to lose their shape, so the active site is no longer a true complementary shape to the substrate. When a substrate molecule collides with an active site it may not fit, so it cannot form an enzyme–substrate complex and is not converted to product.
  5. At about 60 °C, activity stops. The active site is so out of shape that the enzyme has completely stopped working. It is completely denatured.

Explaining the Effect of pH on Enzyme Activity (Supplement)

Something similar happens when pH changes, but the explanation is about shape, not kinetic energy:

  • Each enzyme molecule has a very specific shape, and its active site is the best fit for the substrate at the enzyme's optimum pH.
  • As the pH moves away from the optimum, the enzyme's shape changes, so the active site fits the substrate less well and fewer enzyme–substrate complexes form.
  • A pH well above or below the optimum causes the enzyme molecule to lose its shape completely (denature), so it can no longer bind with the substrate.

B4 Key Formulas and Concepts

  1. Catalyst: a substance that increases the rate of a chemical reaction and is not changed by the reaction; enzymes are biological catalysts made of protein.
  2. How an enzyme works: enzyme + substrate → enzyme–substrate complex → enzyme + product(s)
  3. Amylase: starch → maltose
  4. Maltase: maltose → glucose
  5. Protease: protein → amino acids
  6. Lipase: lipid (fats and oils) → fatty acids + glycerol
  7. Catalase: hydrogen peroxide → water + oxygen
  8. Lactase: lactose → smaller sugars (glucose + galactose), which are absorbed into the blood
  9. Rate and time: rate = 1 ÷ time taken. A shorter time means a faster rate. Example: a time of 4 min gives a rate of 1 ÷ 4 = 0.25 per minute, and a time of 8 min gives 1 ÷ 8 = 0.125 per minute, so the first reaction is twice as fast.
  10. Optimum temperature: about 37 °C for human enzymes; many enzymes are denatured above about 60 °C; some bacteria in hot springs have optimum temperatures up to 80 °C.
  11. Optimum pH: about pH 7 for most enzymes; about pH 2 for the protease in the stomach.
  12. Range: the lowest to the highest value; for example, most enzymes in Figure B4.03b work from about pH 5.6 to about pH 10.
  13. Naming rule: enzyme names usually end in -ase (carbohydrase, protease, lipase, amylase); sugar names end in -ose (maltose, sucrose, lactose).
  14. Iodine test for starch: blue-black means starch is present; brown (the colour of iodine solution itself) means no starch is present.
  15. Thymolphthalein: blue in alkaline conditions; it loses its colour when the pH becomes less alkaline (for example, when fatty acids are made).

Question B4.06

Use your own words to explain the shapes of the curves for most enzymes, in Figure B4.03b.

(In Figure B4.03b, the curve for most enzymes is at zero below about pH 5.6, rises steeply to a peak at about pH 7, and then falls back to zero at about pH 10.)

Answer:

  • At the optimum pH (about pH 7): the enzyme molecule has exactly the right shape, so its active site is the best fit for (complementary to) the substrate. Substrate molecules fit easily, many enzyme–substrate complexes form, and the rate of reaction is highest.
  • As the pH moves away from 7 (more acidic or more alkaline): the change in pH begins to change the shape of the enzyme molecule, including its active site. B5.01 Photosynthesis

Before You Start

The chapter opens with three questions to discuss with a partner, about where the energy in food comes from.

Q: (1) What types of nutrient does your body use to obtain energy?

A: Mainly carbohydrates (such as glucose and starch) and fats (lipids). Proteins can also be used to release energy, but the body mainly uses them for growth and repair.

Q: (2) Energy cannot be created or destroyed. Where did the energy in the nutrients come from originally?

A: From the Sun. The energy was originally light energy in sunlight.

Q: (3) How did the energy get into the nutrients?

A: Plants absorbed the light energy using chlorophyll and transferred it into chemical energy in carbohydrates such as glucose, by photosynthesis. Plants use this glucose to make their other nutrients. Animals take in the energy when they eat plants, or eat other animals that have eaten plants.

Nutrition — Why Plants Are Different from Animals

All living organisms need to take many different substances — nutrients — into their bodies. Some are used to make new parts or to repair old parts. Others are used to release energy. Taking in useful substances is called nutrition.

In Chapter B3 you met the important groups of biological molecules: proteins, fats and carbohydrates. These are all organic substances.

Like all reactions in organisms, photosynthesis depends on enzymes to help it take place.

The Photosynthesis Equation

In photosynthesis, water and carbon dioxide react together to produce glucose and oxygen. They will only do this in the presence of sunlight and chlorophyll.

  • Word equation: carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen (with "sunlight" written above the arrow and "chlorophyll" below it)
  • Balanced chemical equation: 6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂

A balanced chemical equation shows the chemical formula of each reactant and product, and how many molecules of each are involved.

What the formulae tell us:

  • CO₂ (carbon dioxide): one molecule contains 1 atom of carbon (C) and 2 atoms of oxygen (O)
  • H₂O (water): one molecule contains 2 atoms of hydrogen (H) and 1 atom of oxygen
  • C₆H₁₂O₆ (glucose): one molecule contains 6 carbon atoms, 12 hydrogen atoms and 6 oxygen atoms
  • O₂ (oxygen): one molecule contains 2 oxygen atoms

Because a glucose molecule contains 6 carbon atoms, six carbon dioxide molecules are needed to make one glucose molecule. There is the same number of each kind of atom on each side of the equation:

Atom Reactants: 6CO₂ + 6H₂O Products: C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂
Carbon (C) 6 × 1 = 6 6
Hydrogen (H) 6 × 2 = 12 12
Oxygen (O) (6 × 2) + (6 × 1) = 18 6 + (6 × 2) = 18
Reactants (raw materials) Products
Substances Carbon dioxide and water Glucose and oxygen
Where they come from or go Carbon dioxide diffuses in from the air; water comes from the soil Glucose is used or stored by the plant; oxygen is released into the air (or into the water, for an aquatic plant)

Remember: Light and chlorophyll are not reactants. Light provides the energy and chlorophyll captures it, so they are written on the arrow, never after a "+" sign.

Science in Context B5.01: Using Solar Energy to Make Fuels

  • As the human population grows, we use more and more fuel for homes, industries and vehicles. Renewable energy sources such as wind and solar power are being used more, but a lot of our energy still comes from fossil fuels.
  • Burning fossil fuels produces carbon dioxide, so the quantity of carbon dioxide in the atmosphere is increasing. This contributes to global warming, so we urgently need other ways of providing energy.
  • Plants use energy from sunlight to make carbohydrates, which provide energy for their cells. This way of providing energy does not produce carbon dioxide — it uses it up.
  • In many parts of the world, plants are grown to provide biofuels, which are burnt to produce electricity or to move vehicles. But this takes up land that may be needed for food crops, or that would be better left as natural forests or other habitats for wildlife.
  • Scientists are researching artificial photosynthesis to make fuels. Chlorophyll captures energy from sunlight and helps to transfer it into carbohydrates, so researchers are looking for other substances that can do the same job. One laboratory is using tiny particles of gold as a substitute for chlorophyll. It works, but only very slowly. Researchers think it will be at least 2030 before artificial photosynthesis can produce significant quantities of fuel.

Q: How might the large-scale use of artificial photosynthesis help to slow down climate change?

A: Artificial photosynthesis would use energy from sunlight to make fuels from carbon dioxide, so carbon dioxide would be removed from the atmosphere as the fuel is made. When the fuel is burned, it only releases the carbon dioxide that was taken in to make it, so there is no overall increase in atmospheric carbon dioxide. Fossil fuels, by contrast, release carbon that was locked away underground for millions of years. Replacing fossil fuels with these fuels would reduce the build-up of carbon dioxide (a greenhouse gas), slowing global warming and climate change. Unlike biofuel crops, it would not need large areas of land, so forests, which absorb carbon dioxide, could be left standing.

Activity B5.01: Modelling Photosynthesis

Work in a group of four or five. In this model, small balls represent atoms and pieces of egg carton represent molecules.

You will need: egg cartons cut into molecule-shaped pieces (six each of three different shapes, Figure B5.01a–c); one complete egg carton that can hold 12 eggs, labelled to hold a glucose molecule (Figure B5.01d); 36 small balls that fit into the egg cartons (for example, table tennis balls); red, black and blue pens.

Method:

  1. Use the red pen to label 18 of the balls "O" (oxygen), the black pen to label 6 of them "C" (carbon), and the blue pen to label the other 12 "H" (hydrogen).
  2. Take one ball representing oxygen and two balls representing hydrogen, and put them into an angled box to make a water molecule (Figure B5.02).
  3. Make five more water molecules.
  4. Use the remaining balls to make six carbon dioxide molecules, choosing the correct egg carton shapes.
  5. Use your water molecules and carbon dioxide molecules to make a glucose molecule, by putting the balls into the correct holes in the base and lid of the 12-hole egg carton. Use oxygen from the water only — do not use oxygen from the carbon dioxide.
  6. Use your leftover balls to make oxygen molecules.
  7. If you have time, take apart your glucose and oxygen molecules and turn them back into carbon dioxide and water.

How the numbers work: six water molecules use 12 H and 6 O; six carbon dioxide molecules use 6 C and 12 O. The glucose molecule takes 6 C, 12 H and 6 O. The 12 O left over make six O₂ molecules. All 36 balls are used both before and after the "reaction" — the atoms are only rearranged.

Q: (1) Explain how this model represents the balanced equation for photosynthesis.

A: Each ball is an atom and each carton shape is a molecule. You start with six carbon dioxide molecules (6 C and 12 O) and six water molecules (12 H and 6 O), which match the reactants 6CO₂ + 6H₂O. You rearrange exactly the same 36 balls into one glucose molecule (6 C, 12 H, 6 O) and six oxygen molecules (12 O), which match the products C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂. No balls are added or lost, which shows that there is the same number of each type of atom on both sides — the equation is balanced. The numbers of each type of carton show the numbers in front of each formula.

Q: (2) Suggest how you could modify the model, or the way that you use the model, to show how energy in sunlight helps the reaction to take place.

A: For example, only allow the molecules to be taken apart and rebuilt while a torch or lamp ("sunlight") is shining on the group, and only by a group member holding a green card representing chlorophyll. That person could collect "energy tokens" from the lamp and hand one over for each bond that is broken and remade, then place the tokens inside the glucose carton. This shows that chlorophyll captures light energy and that the energy ends up stored in glucose.

Q: (3) You may hear people say that "photosynthesis changes carbon dioxide into oxygen". Use your model to explain why this is not correct.

A: In the model, no ball ever changes into a different kind of ball — a carbon atom stays a carbon atom. The carbon dioxide molecules are broken apart and their atoms are rearranged: the carbon atoms from carbon dioxide end up in the glucose molecule, not in oxygen. Water is also a reactant, and its hydrogen atoms end up in glucose too. So photosynthesis turns carbon dioxide and water into glucose and oxygen; carbon dioxide is not simply "changed into" oxygen. (Beyond the syllabus: experiments with labelled atoms show that, in real plants, the oxygen gas released actually comes from the water molecules, so the model is a simplification.)

Chlorophyll — Capturing Energy from Sunlight

In the chloroplasts of a plant's leaves, water and carbon dioxide react together to make carbohydrates and oxygen. In other circumstances, you could shine light on a mixture of carbon dioxide and water for ever, and no carbohydrates or oxygen would ever appear. What is special about a plant's leaves is that they contain a green pigment called chlorophyll, which is present in their chloroplasts.

How chlorophyll works, step by step:

  1. Chlorophyll captures energy from sunlight (Figure B5.03).
  2. It immediately passes the energy on (transfers it) to water molecules and carbon dioxide molecules.
  3. The energy makes these substances react, producing a kind of carbohydrate called glucose (and oxygen).
  4. The glucose made in the leaves contains some of the energy that was originally in the sunlight.

So chlorophyll transfers energy from light into energy in carbohydrates (light energy becomes chemical energy). Figure B5.03 shows a forest described as "a giant carbohydrate factory", using sunlight as fuel and carbon dioxide and water as its raw materials.

How a Plant Uses Carbohydrates

Photosynthesis has two products: glucose and oxygen.

  • The oxygen is released into the atmosphere (or into the water, if the plant is aquatic).
  • The glucose (a carbohydrate) is used for many different purposes, described below.

Releasing Useful Energy

Some of the glucose is used to provide energy for activities that the plant's cells need to carry out. For example:

  • Energy is needed to move mineral ions into the root hairs by active transport.
  • Energy is needed to build protein molecules from amino acids, for growth.

The energy is released from glucose by respiration (Chapter B9).

Storing, to Use Later — Starch

Plants usually make much more glucose than they need to use for energy immediately. They store it by turning it into starch.

  • Starch molecules are made by linking many glucose molecules together into a long, spiral chain.
  • Millions of these molecules clump together to make starch grains. Figure B5.04 is a photomicrograph of potato tuber cells; iodine solution has been added, so the starch grains are stained blue.

Why starch is a good storage substance:

  • Starch molecules are not soluble in water, so they do not get involved in the chemical reactions taking place inside the cells.
  • They do not affect the concentration of the solutions inside the cell, so they do not cause water to enter or leave by osmosis.
  • They can be quickly and easily broken down to glucose again when the plant needs it.

Making Sucrose, for Transport

  • Plants can only make glucose in the parts that contain chlorophyll, which usually means the leaves. All the other parts of the plant must have carbohydrate delivered to them.
  • Plants do not actually transport glucose. They first change it into a sugar with larger molecules, called sucrose.
  • Sucrose is carried from one part of the plant to another inside phloem tubes (Chapter B7).
  • When the sucrose reaches its destination, it can be changed back to glucose and used in respiration to release energy.

Making Cellulose, to Build Cell Walls

  • As plants grow, they make new cells. Every cell needs a cell wall, so a growing plant must make cellulose molecules to form these walls.
  • Cellulose is made by linking glucose molecules in long chains. The molecules are linked in a different way from starch, so the chains stay straight rather than coiling up into spirals.

Making Nectar, to Attract Pollinators

  • Many plants reproduce sexually, producing male and female gametes in flowers.
  • Unlike animal gametes, the male gametes of flowers cannot move themselves from place to place. Instead, they rely on insects, bats or birds to carry them, inside pollen grains, from one flower to another.
  • No animal will do this unless it gets a reward, so flowers produce nectar that animals can feed on. Nectar contains different kinds of sugar, all made from the glucose that the plant has made by photosynthesis.
  • Figure B5.05 shows insects visiting flowers to collect carbohydrate-rich nectar and protein-rich pollen. Without knowing it, they help the flower by pollinating it.

Making Amino Acids, to Make Proteins

  • Plants use some of the glucose made in photosynthesis to make amino acids, which are then used to make proteins, for growth.
  • Proteins contain carbon, hydrogen and oxygen, and also nitrogen. So to make amino acids from glucose, plants need a source of nitrogen.
  • They get nitrogen from the soil in the form of nitrate ions. These are usually taken in by active transport through the root hairs, then transported to all parts of the plant, where they are combined with glucose to make amino acids.
  • If a plant cannot get enough nitrate ions, it cannot synthesise proteins effectively, so it will not grow quickly or strongly.

Making Other Substances, such as Chlorophyll

  • Glucose can also be used to make chlorophyll.
  • Chlorophyll is not a protein, but it does contain nitrogen. It also contains magnesium. Therefore plants need to take in magnesium ions, as well as nitrate ions, to make chlorophyll.
  • Without these ions, the plant's leaves look yellow rather than green. Figure B5.06 shows leaves on an orange tree growing in soil that does not contain enough magnesium ions; the tree cannot make enough chlorophyll, so the leaves are not completely green.
  • If a plant cannot make lots of chlorophyll, it cannot photosynthesise well, and so it will not grow well.

B5.02 Leaves

A Leaf Is a Carbohydrate Factory

Photosynthesis happens inside chloroplasts. This is where the enzymes and chlorophyll that catalyse and supply energy for the reaction are found. In a typical plant, most chloroplasts are in the cells in the leaves, so a leaf is a factory for making carbohydrates. It is not surprising that most plants have leaves that are perfectly adapted to help photosynthesis take place as quickly and efficiently as possible.

The Structure of a Leaf

Figure B5.07a shows the parts of a leaf:

  • Lamina — the broad, flat part of the leaf.
  • Leaf stalk — joins the lamina to the rest of the plant. Inside the leaf stalk are collections of parallel tubes called vascular bundles.
  • Midrib — the main vein running down the middle of the lamina.
  • Veins — the vascular bundles branching through the lamina. The tubes in the vascular bundles carry substances to and from the leaf (Chapter B7).

Figure B5.07a also shows where a transverse section of the leaf and a cross-section of a vein are cut. Figure B5.07b shows the network of veins in a dicot leaf, which carries water to all parts of it.

Inside the leaf cells, photosynthesis happens in chloroplasts. The chlorophyll is spread out on membranes inside the chloroplasts, so that a lot of sunlight can reach it.

Why Most Leaves Are Broad and Thin

The raw materials for photosynthesis are carbon dioxide and water, and sunlight is needed to provide energy. Most — but not all — leaves have a large surface area and are very thin.

Feature How it helps photosynthesis
Large surface area Allows large amounts of sunlight to fall onto the leaf; also increases the rate at which carbon dioxide can diffuse into the leaf from the air
Thin Sunlight can pass right through the leaf, so many cells inside it can photosynthesise; carbon dioxide can reach all the cells quite quickly by diffusion
Network of veins Xylem vessels in the veins bring water from the soil to all parts of the leaf

Only 0.04% of the air is carbon dioxide, so the structure of a leaf must allow carbon dioxide to move into the leaf really easily.

Water is brought to the leaf from the soil in tubes called xylem vessels (pronounced "zi-lem"). These run in the vascular bundles, which form the veins in the leaf. You can often see many tiny veins if you look closely at a leaf.

How the Raw Materials Reach a Palisade Cell

Figure B5.08 summarises how the raw materials for photosynthesis travel to a chloroplast in a palisade cell:

Sunlight (energy) Passes through the transparent cuticle and upper epidermis
Carbon dioxide Diffuses through the stomata from the atmosphere, then diffuses through the air spaces to the palisade cells
Water Travels from the roots in xylem vessels, then travels to the chloroplasts by osmosis

Tissues in a Leaf

Although a leaf is thin, it is made up of several layers of cells. You can see these if you look at a transverse section (TS) of a leaf under a microscope (Figure B5.09). Each tissue in a leaf has its own function. From top to bottom:

Tissue or structure What it is like Function and adaptation
Cuticle A waxy substance forming a thin, transparent, waterproof covering Reduces water loss; transparent, so light passes straight through
Upper epidermis A layer of transparent cells packed tightly together, with no chloroplasts Reduces the quantity of water vapour escaping from the leaf; lets light through to the palisade cells
Palisade mesophyll Tall, narrow cells containing very large numbers of chloroplasts, close to the top of the leaf Main function is photosynthesis; being near the top, they get plenty of sunlight through the transparent epidermis above
Spongy mesophyll Rounded, loosely packed cells with chloroplasts and many air spaces between them Also photosynthesise; the air spaces let carbon dioxide and oxygen diffuse between the air and the cells inside the leaf, and let water vapour move from the cell surfaces to the outside of the leaf
Vascular bundle (vein) Xylem vessels and phloem tubes side by side Xylem brings water and mineral ions into the leaf; phloem carries sucrose away to the rest of the plant
Lower epidermis A layer of cells that usually has no cuticle The underside of the leaf does not often have sunlight falling on it, so it does not get as hot and does not lose as much water vapour
Stomata Openings in the lower epidermis Allow diffusion of carbon dioxide and oxygen in and out of the leaf; water vapour also diffuses out through them
Guard cells A pair of cells around each stoma; they contain chloroplasts Change their shape to open and close the stomata

The mesophyll layer is made up of the palisade mesophyll and spongy mesophyll together. Figure B5.09 also shows the inside of a palisade cell: a cell wall, cytoplasm, a nucleus, a large vacuole and many chloroplasts.

The Palisade Mesophyll Cell

The palisade mesophyll cell is the leaf cell that is best adapted for photosynthesis:

  • It contains very large numbers of chloroplasts, so lots of chlorophyll is available to absorb light energy.
  • It is tall and narrow, and the cells are packed side by side in a layer, so many cells fit into the top part of the leaf where light is brightest.
  • It is close to the upper surface, just below the transparent cuticle and upper epidermis, so it gets plenty of sunlight.
  • It is close to air spaces (supplying carbon dioxide by diffusion) and to the veins (supplying water, which moves to the chloroplasts by osmosis).

Identifying Leaf Structures in Photomicrographs

You need to identify leaf structures in diagrams and in photomicrographs of a dicotyledonous leaf, such as Figure B5.10 (part of a tea plant leaf, ×400). Tips for recognising each layer:

  • Upper epidermis — the single row of cells forming the top surface; the cuticle is the thin line on top of it.
  • Palisade mesophyll — the row of tall, narrow cells packed tightly side by side just below the upper epidermis; they look darker because they are full of chloroplasts.
  • Spongy mesophyll — rounded, irregular cells with many spaces between them, in the lower part of the leaf.
  • Vascular bundle — a cluster of cells (xylem and phloem), often near the middle of the leaf.
  • Lower epidermis — the row of cells on the bottom surface. Look for a gap (a stoma) between two small guard cells, usually with a large air space just above it.

B5.03 Factors Affecting Photosynthesis

What Affects the Rate of Photosynthesis?

The rate of photosynthesis is how fast it happens — for example, how much oxygen is given off in unit time. The rate is affected by several environmental factors:

  • The supply of the raw materials — carbon dioxide and water
  • The quantity of sunlight, which provides energy for the reactions
  • The temperature, because this affects the activity of enzymes

As well as these environmental factors, the quantity of chlorophyll in the leaf also helps to determine how fast photosynthesis can take place.

Factor Effect of increasing it Reason
Light intensity Rate increases, until another factor limits it More energy is available for the reactions
Carbon dioxide concentration Rate increases, until another factor limits it More of a raw material is available
Temperature Rate increases up to an optimum, then falls quickly Enzymes work faster as temperature rises, but are denatured at high temperatures
Quantity of chlorophyll More chlorophyll allows a faster rate More light energy can be absorbed

Limiting Factors

Suppose a plant has plenty of light and a warm temperature, but very little carbon dioxide. Making the light brighter will not make it photosynthesise any faster, because the shortage of carbon dioxide is holding the rate back. The factor that is in shortest supply, and is holding back the rate, is called the limiting factor.

  • On a graph of rate against a factor, the line rises while that factor is limiting, then levels off (plateaus) when some other factor becomes limiting.
  • This explains why glasshouse growers add extra heat, light or carbon dioxide (Project B5.01), and why the students in Practice Question 6 put their syringes in bright sunshine: so that light would not limit the rate and they could see the effect of carbon dioxide.

Experimental Skills B5.01: Testing a Leaf for Starch

Aim: To find out whether a leaf contains starch. Leaves turn some of the glucose that they make by photosynthesis into starch, so if we find starch in a leaf, that tells us it has been photosynthesising. You also practise working safely, making careful observations and drawing conclusions.

You will need: a 250 cm³ beaker; a burner, tripod and gauze; a boiling tube; some iodine solution, with a dropper; forceps; a glass rod; some alcohol (methylated spirits) — collect this only when you are ready to use it; a fresh green leaf that has been photosynthesising; a white tile.

Safety:

  • Alcohol is flammable (burns easily).
  • Do not collect the alcohol until you have turned off your burner.
  • If your alcohol (or someone else's) does start to burn, put a damp cloth over it immediately.

Method (Figure B5.11):

  1. Boil some water in a beaker. Take a green leaf from a healthy plant and drop it into the boiling water. This breaks down the cell membranes in the leaf. Leave the leaf in the boiling water for about 30 seconds.
  2. Turn off the flame.
  3. Put some alcohol into a boiling tube. Stand the tube of alcohol in the hot water.
  4. Use the forceps to remove the softened leaf from the hot water and drop it into the tube of alcohol. Chlorophyll is soluble in alcohol, so it comes out of the leaf. Leave the leaf in the alcohol until all the chlorophyll has come out.
  5. The leaf will now be brittle (it will break easily). Carefully remove it from the alcohol, and dip it into hot water again to soften it.
  6. Spread out the leaf on a white tile and cover it with iodine solution. A blue-black colour shows that the leaf contains starch.
Step Why it is done
Boil the leaf in water Breaks down the cell membranes so that iodine solution can reach the starch inside the cells; it also kills the cells
Turn off the flame before using alcohol Alcohol is flammable and must never be near a naked flame
Stand the tube of alcohol in hot water Warms the alcohol safely so it dissolves the chlorophyll faster
Leave the leaf in alcohol Removes the green chlorophyll, so the leaf goes pale and colour changes are easy to see
Dip the leaf in hot water again Softens the brittle leaf so it can be spread out without breaking
Use a white tile Makes the colour of the leaf easy to see
Add iodine solution Turns blue-black where starch is present; stays orange-brown where there is none

Variables: Nothing is varied — this is a standard test that you will use in Experimental skills B5.02, B5.03 and B5.04.

Expected results: A leaf from a plant that has been photosynthesising turns blue-black. Any part without starch stays the orange-brown colour of the iodine solution.

Conclusion: A blue-black leaf contains starch, so it has been photosynthesising.

Q: (1) Describe the results of your experiment. What conclusion can you make?

A: When iodine solution was added, the pale leaf turned blue-black. (Iodine solution itself is orange-brown, and it would have stayed orange-brown if there was no starch.) Conclusion: the leaf contained starch, so it had been photosynthesising.

Q: (2) Cell membranes are partially permeable. They will not allow starch or iodine molecules to pass through them. Use this information to explain why it was necessary to boil the leaf.

A: The starch is inside the cells, surrounded by cell membranes. Iodine molecules cannot pass through intact cell membranes, so the iodine solution could not reach the starch and no colour change would be seen. Boiling the leaf breaks down the cell membranes, so the iodine solution can get into the cells and react with the starch.

Q: (3) Suggest why it is a good idea to remove the chlorophyll from the leaf, before adding iodine solution to it.

A: The dark green colour of chlorophyll would hide (mask) the colour of the iodine solution, so it would be difficult to tell whether the leaf had turned blue-black. Removing the chlorophyll leaves the leaf pale, so the colour change is easy to see.

Experimental Skills B5.02: Investigating the Necessity for Chlorophyll for Photosynthesis

Aim: To find out whether chlorophyll is needed for photosynthesis, using the starch test on a variegated leaf — a leaf that has some white areas and some green areas (Figure B5.12). You also practise recording observations with a labelled drawing.

You will need: the same apparatus as for Experimental skills B5.01 Testing a leaf for starch; a fresh variegated leaf, taken from a plant that has been photosynthesising.

Safety: Alcohol is flammable (burns easily). Do not collect the alcohol until you have turned off your burner. If your alcohol (or someone else's) does start to burn, put a damp cloth over it immediately.

Method:

  1. Make a drawing of your variegated leaf. Do not colour it. Label the parts that are green and the parts that are white.
  2. Test the leaf for starch, following the procedure described in Experimental skills B5.01.
  3. Record your results by drawing and labelling the leaf after you have added iodine solution to it.
Variable or feature In this investigation
Independent variable Whether chlorophyll is present (green areas) or absent (white areas)
Dependent variable Whether starch is present (colour with iodine solution)
Controlled variables Both areas are on the same leaf, so they had the same light, carbon dioxide, water, temperature and time
Control The green parts of the leaf

Expected results: The areas that were green turn blue-black; the areas that were white stay orange-brown. The pattern of blue-black on the second drawing matches the pattern of green on the first drawing.

Conclusion: Only the parts of the leaf that contain chlorophyll made starch, so chlorophyll is necessary for photosynthesis.

Q: (1) Which parts of the leaf contained starch?

A: Only the parts that were green (containing chlorophyll) — they turned blue-black. The white parts stayed orange-brown, so they contained no starch.

Q: (2) Write a brief conclusion for your experiment.

A: Starch was only made in the green parts of the leaf, which contain chlorophyll. The white parts, which have no chlorophyll, did not make starch. This shows that chlorophyll is necessary for photosynthesis.

Experimental Skills B5.03: Investigating the Necessity for Light for Photosynthesis

Aim: To use the starch test to compare the starch content of parts of a leaf that have received light with parts that have not. You also practise recording results with a labelled drawing and writing a short conclusion.

You will need: a healthy plant growing in a pot, which has been in a dark cupboard for two days; some black paper, scissors and paper clips; (after one day) the same apparatus as for Experimental skills B5.01 Testing a leaf for starch.

Safety: Alcohol is flammable (burns easily). Do not collect the alcohol until you have turned off your burner. If your alcohol (or someone else's) does start to burn, put a damp cloth over it immediately.

Method:

  1. Cut out a shape from the black paper, which you can attach to a leaf. If you like, you can cut your initials into the paper (Figure B5.13).
  2. Attach the paper to a leaf on the potted plant. Do not take the leaf off the plant!
  3. Put the plant into a place where it gets plenty of light. Leave it until the next day.
  4. Remove the leaf from the plant. Remove the black paper from the leaf.
  5. Test the leaf for starch.
  6. Record your results by drawing and labelling the leaf after you have added iodine solution to it.

Destarching: Before the experiment, the plant was kept in a dark cupboard for two days. With no light it could not photosynthesise, so it used up its starch stores. Destarching means leaving a plant in the dark for long enough for it to use up its starch stores.

Variable or feature In this investigation
Independent variable Whether the part of the leaf received light (uncovered) or not (covered by black paper)
Dependent variable Whether starch is present (colour with iodine solution)
Controlled variables Same leaf on the same plant, so the same chlorophyll, carbon dioxide, water, temperature and time
Control The part of the leaf that was not covered

Expected results: The part covered by black paper stays orange-brown (no starch). The uncovered part turns blue-black (starch). If you cut your initials into the paper, light shone through the holes, so your initials show up blue-black on an orange-brown background.

Conclusion: Only the parts of the leaf that received light made starch, so light is necessary for photosynthesis.

Why only part of the leaf is covered: If you had covered all of the leaf and found that none of it had photosynthesised, you would not be sure that the reason was because it had no light. It could have been because the whole plant was not photosynthesising.

Q: (1) The potted plant was put into a cupboard to make sure it used up all its starch stores and had no light to be able to make more. This is called destarching. Suggest why this was a necessary part of this experiment.

A: Destarching makes sure there is no starch in the leaf at the start. Then any starch found at the end must have been made by photosynthesis during the experiment, in the parts that received light. If the leaf still contained old starch, the covered part might test positive even though it had not photosynthesised, and you could not tell whether light is needed.

Q: (2) Explain why, in this experiment, the leaf had to remain attached to the plant for a day, after you put the black paper onto it.

A: The leaf needed time in the light to photosynthesise and make enough starch to be detected by the iodine test. It had to stay attached to the plant so that it stayed alive and healthy and kept receiving water (through the xylem from the roots), which is a raw material for photosynthesis. A leaf removed from the plant would wilt and would not photosynthesise normally.

Q: (3) Use your results to write a short conclusion to your experiment.

A: The part of the leaf covered by black paper did not contain starch (it stayed orange-brown), but the uncovered part did (it turned blue-black). This shows that light is necessary for photosynthesis.

Controls

A control is a standard sample that you use as a comparison, to find the effect of changing a variable. The control is the same as the test sample in every way except for the one factor being investigated.

  • In Experimental skills B5.02, you wanted to find out whether chlorophyll is needed for photosynthesis. The white parts of the leaf had no chlorophyll, and you could compare them with the normal, green parts. The green parts were the control.
  • In Experimental skills B5.03, you wanted to find out whether light is needed. You needed to compare a part of a leaf that did have light with a part that did not. The part of the leaf that you did not cover was the control. It allowed you to compare the part that did not have light with this normal part, which did.

Experimental Skills B5.04: Investigating the Necessity for Carbon Dioxide for Photosynthesis

Aim: To compare the ability of two leaves on the same plant — one with carbon dioxide and one without — to make starch. This is not an easy experiment to set up, so you will probably use one set of apparatus for the whole class.

You will need: a healthy plant growing in a pot, which has been destarched (see Experimental skills B5.03); two transparent containers, e.g. conical flasks; two rubber bungs to fit the flasks, each with a slit in one edge in which a leaf stalk can fit; petroleum jelly; a method of supporting the transparent containers; some potassium hydroxide solution; some distilled water; (after one day) the same apparatus as for Experimental skills B5.01 Testing a leaf for starch.

Safety: Alcohol is flammable (burns easily). Do not collect the alcohol until you have turned off your burner. If your alcohol (or someone else's) does start to burn, put a damp cloth over it immediately. Potassium hydroxide solution is also corrosive, so wear eye protection and avoid skin contact.

Method (Figure B5.14):

  1. Set up the apparatus as shown in Figure B5.14. Find a way of supporting the flasks so that they do not pull on the leaves — you could use a retort stand and clamps. Put potassium hydroxide solution into one flask and distilled water into the other. Hold one of the leaf stalks carefully, slot it into the slit in one of the rubber bungs, then gently push the flask over the bung and into its support. Repeat with the other leaf and flask.
  2. Rub petroleum jelly over the rubber bungs, to try to prevent any air entering or leaving the flasks (an airtight seal).
  3. Place the apparatus in a light place and leave it for at least one day.
  4. Test both leaves for starch.
  5. Record your results.

Figure B5.14 shows a potted plant with two of its leaves (still attached) each sealed inside a flask: one flask contains potassium hydroxide solution to absorb carbon dioxide, and the other contains distilled water.

Variable or feature In this investigation
Independent variable Whether carbon dioxide is present (distilled water flask) or absent (potassium hydroxide flask)
Dependent variable Whether starch is present in each leaf (colour with iodine solution)
Controlled variables Same plant, same light, same temperature, same time; both leaves sealed in a flask containing a liquid
Control The leaf in the flask containing distilled water

Expected results: The leaf in the flask with potassium hydroxide solution stays orange-brown (no starch). The leaf in the flask with distilled water turns blue-black (starch).

Conclusion: The leaf without carbon dioxide could not make starch, so carbon dioxide is necessary for photosynthesis.

Q: (1) What was the control in this experiment?

A: The leaf in the flask containing distilled water. It had carbon dioxide in the air around it, but was otherwise treated in exactly the same way as the leaf in the potassium hydroxide flask.

Q: (2) Suggest why distilled water was added to one flask, rather than just leaving it empty.

A: So that the two flasks were the same in every way except for the presence of carbon dioxide. Potassium hydroxide solution is mostly water, so it makes the air in its flask humid. Putting the same volume of distilled water (which does not absorb carbon dioxide) in the other flask keeps the conditions, such as humidity, the same. Then any difference between the two leaves can only be caused by the carbon dioxide.

Q: (3) Write a short conclusion for your experiment.

A: The leaf in the flask with potassium hydroxide solution (no carbon dioxide) did not contain starch, while the leaf in the flask with distilled water (carbon dioxide present) did contain starch. This shows that carbon dioxide is necessary for photosynthesis.

Experimental Skills B5.05: Investigating the Effect of Varying Light Intensity on the Rate of Photosynthesis

Aim: To find out how light intensity affects the rate of photosynthesis, using an aquatic plant (a plant that lives in water). An aquatic plant makes it easy to measure the rate, because you can see bubbles of oxygen being released into the water. To measure a rate, you need to measure how much oxygen is given off in unit time — a definite length of time, such as one minute.

You will need: a healthy aquatic plant, which has been in the light and has been photosynthesising; the apparatus shown in Figure B5.15; a paperclip; a lamp; a ruler to measure the distance between the lamp and the plant; a stopwatch or timer.

Safety: Keep water away from the lamp.

Before you start: Check that you know how to use your stopwatch or timer, and that it is working properly.

Method:

  1. Collect a piece of aquatic plant. Cut off a piece about 7 to 9 cm long. Work out which way up it is. Attach a paperclip to the top of it.
  2. Set up the apparatus as shown in Figure B5.15. The paperclip will hold the top of the plant down, so that its cut end floats upwards.
  3. Place the lamp as close as possible to the beaker. Measure the distance, and record it in a results table.
  4. Start the stopwatch or timer. Count the number of bubbles released from the cut end of the stem in one minute. Record this, and then repeat two more times.
  5. Move the lamp a little bit further from the plant. Measure the new distance, and repeat step 4.
  6. Repeat step 5 several times, until you have bubble counts for at least five different distances. Do more if you have time.
  7. Plot your results as a line graph, with distance on the x-axis and mean number of bubbles on the y-axis. Make sure that the two axes are labelled fully, and that you choose suitable scales. Join the points using ruled, straight lines between the points.

Figure B5.15 shows a lamp on a stand at one end of a metre rule, a transparent heat shield, and a beaker of water containing the cut stem of the aquatic plant, with bubbles containing oxygen produced in photosynthesis rising from the cut end.

The results table in the book has these columns (the last four are all under the heading "Number of bubbles produced in one minute"):

Distance of lamp from beaker / cm 1st try 2nd try 3rd try Mean

To work out each mean, add the three counts and divide by 3. For example, counts of 24, 26 and 25 bubbles give a mean of (24 + 26 + 25) ÷ 3 = 25 bubbles per minute.

Variable or feature In this investigation
Independent variable Light intensity, changed by moving the lamp to different distances from the beaker
Dependent variable Rate of photosynthesis, measured as the number of bubbles released from the cut stem in one minute
Controlled variables Temperature (kept steady by the heat shield); carbon dioxide concentration in the water; the same piece of plant; the same lamp; the same counting time; other light in the room kept low
Repeats Three counts at each distance, then a mean is calculated

Expected results: The closer the lamp, the more bubbles are produced per minute. As the lamp is moved further away, the number of bubbles per minute decreases, so the line on the graph slopes downwards from left to right.

Conclusion: Light intensity decreases as the lamp is moved further away, so the results show that the higher the light intensity, the faster the rate of photosynthesis.

Q: (1) Explain why an aquatic plant was used in this experiment.

A: An aquatic plant releases the oxygen made in photosynthesis into the water as visible bubbles, which can be counted (or collected). This makes it easy to measure how much oxygen is given off in unit time, which is the rate of photosynthesis. A land plant releases oxygen into the air, where it cannot be seen or easily measured.

Q: (2) Light intensity decreases as the distance of the lamp from the beaker increases. Write a conclusion to summarise what your results show about the effect of light intensity on the rate of photosynthesis.

A: As the lamp was moved further from the beaker, the mean number of bubbles produced per minute decreased. Because light intensity decreases with distance, this shows that the higher the light intensity, the faster the rate of photosynthesis. Light provides the energy for photosynthesis, so more light energy allows the reaction to happen faster.

Q: (3) Lamps produce heat as well as light. Explain why you would be unable to draw a conclusion about light intensity, if you did not use a heat shield.

A: Without a heat shield, the lamp would warm the water more when it was close to the beaker than when it was far away. Temperature also affects the rate of photosynthesis, because it changes the activity of enzymes. So light intensity and temperature would both be changing at the same time, and you could not tell whether a change in the number of bubbles was caused by the light or by the heat. The transparent heat shield lets light through but stops most of the heat reaching the plant, so light intensity is the only variable that changes.

Q: (4) It is possible to collect the gas given off by the plant and measure its volume. Suggest why this would be an improvement on the method you have used here.

A: Bubbles are not all the same size, so counting them does not measure the amount of oxygen accurately. Bubbles may also come off too fast to count, and very small bubbles may be missed. Collecting the gas (for example in an upturned measuring cylinder, a gas syringe or a capillary tube) and measuring its volume in cm³ gives a more accurate measurement of how much gas is produced per minute.

Self Assessment: Drawing a Good Line Graph

After drawing your graph, give yourself a mark for each feature below: 2 marks if you did it really well; 1 mark if you made a good attempt at it and partly succeeded; 0 marks if you did not try to do it, or did not succeed.

  • The axes are both fully labelled, including units.
  • The scales on both axes go up in even steps.
  • The scales use at least half of the graph paper.
  • The points are plotted as small, neat crosses in exactly the right place.
  • The lines between the points are drawn with a ruler, and exactly meet the centres of the crosses.

Experimental Skills B5.06: Investigating the Effect of Temperature and Carbon Dioxide on the Rate of Photosynthesis

Aim: To plan and carry out your own experiment, using a method similar to Experimental skills B5.05, but with either carbon dioxide concentration or temperature as the independent variable.

You will need: the apparatus used in Experimental skills B5.05; electric water-baths, or access to ice and hot water, and a thermometer.

Method:

  1. Think about how you will vary temperature. You can stand the beaker in a water-bath at different temperatures, or add ice and hot water to the water around the plant. Aim to have at least five values of your independent variable.
  2. Think about other variables that you should try to keep constant, and how you will do this.
  3. Write out your plan. You could structure it like this: the independent variable and how you will vary it; the values of the independent variable you will use; the variables that you will keep constant, and how you will do this; the variable that you will measure, and how you will do this; possible safety risks, and how you will minimise them; an outline results chart, ready to complete as you work.
  4. If possible, exchange your plan with another person in your class who is investigating the other variable. Give them feedback on their plan, and use their feedback to make improvements to your own plan.
  5. When you are ready, carry out your experiment and record your results.

A: Put the independent variable on the x-axis, labelled "Temperature / °C" or "Concentration of sodium hydrogencarbonate solution / %". Put "Mean number of bubbles per minute" on the y-axis. Use even scales that fill at least half of the grid, plot each mean as a small, neat cross, and join the crosses with ruled straight lines. For temperature, the line should rise to a peak at the optimum and then fall steeply. For carbon dioxide, the line should rise and then level off.

Q: (2) Write a short conclusion.

A: Temperature: "As the temperature increased, the rate of photosynthesis increased up to an optimum temperature (read the exact value from your graph), and then decreased. This is because the enzymes that catalyse photosynthesis work faster as temperature rises, but are denatured at high temperatures." Carbon dioxide: "As the carbon dioxide concentration increased, the rate of photosynthesis increased, because more of this raw material was available. At the highest concentrations the rate levelled off, because another factor, such as light intensity, became limiting."

Q: (3) You probably encountered some difficulties or problems as you did your experiment. What were they, and how did you overcome them?

A: Typical problems and solutions:

  • The water temperature drifted during counting — use a thermostatically controlled electric water-bath and check the temperature with a thermometer before and after each count.
  • Bubbles came off too fast, unevenly or in different sizes — count for longer, or collect the gas and measure its volume.
  • The plant took time to adjust after each change — wait a few minutes at each new temperature or concentration before starting to count.
  • The lamp warmed the water — use a heat shield and keep the lamp at a fixed distance.
  • Different pieces of plant gave different results — use the same piece of plant throughout.

Q: (4) If you could start again, and had lots of time available, what improvements would you make to your experiment?

A: For example: measure the volume of gas produced instead of counting bubbles (or use an oxygen sensor and data logger); test more values over a wider range, with smaller steps near the optimum; repeat each measurement at least three times, calculate means and repeat any anomalous results; control all the other variables more strictly; and give the plant a few minutes to adjust to each new condition before measuring.

Project B5.01: Designing a Glasshouse

Aim: To design a glasshouse to grow a crop in your country. The glasshouse will provide ideal conditions to help the plants photosynthesise quickly and grow fast, so that they give a high yield for the grower.

Method: Work in a group of three or four. You might like to assign different roles to different people (e.g. organiser, researcher, communicator). Research the questions below, then give a presentation to the rest of the class. You can make your presentation as a video, use PowerPoint, or make models and draw diagrams — or all of these.

Variables to control: temperature, light intensity, carbon dioxide concentration, water supply and mineral ions — each should be kept at a level where it does not limit the rate of photosynthesis, without costing more than the extra crop is worth.

Safety considerations: Burners used to heat the glasshouse and supply carbon dioxide must be well maintained and ventilated, because faulty burners can produce poisonous carbon monoxide. Keep lamps, heaters and electrical cables away from water sprays and irrigation.

Q: What crop will you grow in the glasshouse?

A: Choose a valuable crop that grows well under cover, for example tomatoes, cucumbers, peppers, lettuce, strawberries or flowers.

Q: What conditions does this crop need to grow well?

A: Plenty of light; warm (but not too hot) temperatures — about 20–25 °C for tomatoes during the day; enough carbon dioxide; a steady supply of water; mineral ions such as nitrate ions (for proteins) and magnesium ions (for chlorophyll); and protection from pests and diseases.

Q: How can a glasshouse provide better conditions for the crop, than if it was grown outside?

A: Glass lets light in and traps heat, so it is warmer inside. Temperature, light, carbon dioxide, water and mineral ions can all be controlled, so that none of them limits the rate of photosynthesis. The crop is also protected from wind, frost, heavy rain and many pests, and it can be grown out of season or all year round.

Q: How will you control temperature in the glasshouse? In your country, will you need to heat it, cool it, or do both at different times?

A: To heat it: use electric heaters or paraffin or gas burners (which also release carbon dioxide), and insulate it at night. To cool it: open vents and windows, use fans, shade the glass, or spray a fine water mist. A thermostat can switch these on and off to keep the temperature near the crop's optimum. A cold country mainly needs heating, a hot country mainly needs cooling, and many countries need heating at night or in winter and cooling on hot days.

Q: How will you control light in the glasshouse? Will you need to give extra light, or might you need to shade it sometimes?

A: Lamps (such as LED grow lights) can raise the light intensity on dull days and lengthen the day in winter, so photosynthesis continues for longer. In very sunny, hot places, blinds, shade netting or whitewash on the glass can reduce the light so that the plants do not overheat or lose too much water.

Q: How will you provide carbon dioxide in the glasshouse? Providing it costs money, so what might be a suitable concentration to aim for, that will give a good return for your expenditure?

A: Burn paraffin, propane or natural gas (which supplies heat as well), or release carbon dioxide from cylinders. Normal air contains about 0.04% carbon dioxide. Raising it to about 0.1% (about two and a half times normal) greatly increases the rate of photosynthesis. Above about 0.1%, the rate increases very little because another factor, such as light or temperature, becomes limiting, so the extra cost would not be repaid by a bigger crop.

Expected outcome: A good design keeps every factor close to its best level, so the crop photosynthesises quickly, grows fast and gives a high yield that is worth more than the cost of heating, lighting and carbon dioxide. Remember water (irrigation or drip-feeding) and fertilisers containing nitrate and magnesium ions too.

B5 Key Formulas and Concepts

  1. Word equation for photosynthesis: carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen (in the presence of sunlight and chlorophyll)
  2. Balanced chemical equation: 6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂
  3. The equation balances: 6 carbon, 12 hydrogen and 18 oxygen atoms on each side
  4. 6 molecules of CO₂ and 6 of H₂O make 1 molecule of glucose and 6 of O₂, so number of glucose molecules = number of CO₂ molecules ÷ 6 (e.g. 12 ÷ 6 = 2)
  5. Chlorophyll absorbs light energy and transfers it into chemical energy stored in glucose
  6. Glucose + nitrogen (from nitrate ions) → amino acids → proteins
  7. Glucose + nitrogen (from nitrate ions) + magnesium (from magnesium ions) → chlorophyll
  8. Glucose is used in respiration (energy) and converted to starch (storage), sucrose (transport in phloem), cellulose (cell walls) and nectar (attracting pollinators)
  9. Carbon dioxide route: atmosphere → stomata → air spaces → palisade cells (by diffusion)
  10. Water route: soil → roots → xylem vessels in the veins → leaf cells (by osmosis)
  11. Starch test: boil in water, then warm alcohol (water bath, no flame), then soften in hot water, then add iodine solution: blue-black = starch, orange-brown = no starch
  12. Rate of photosynthesis = quantity of oxygen produced ÷ time (e.g. bubbles per minute, or cm³ of gas per minute)
  13. Mean = (1st try + 2nd try + 3rd try) ÷ 3
  14. Light intensity decreases as the lamp is moved further away (useful extension: light intensity is proportional to 1 ÷ distance²)
  15. Air contains about 0.04% carbon dioxide; glasshouse growers often raise it to about 0.1%
  16. Rate rises with light intensity and carbon dioxide concentration until another factor limits it; rate rises with temperature up to an optimum, then falls as enzymes are denatured

B5 Textbook Questions with Answers

Question B5.01

Explain why animals are dependent on plants for their supply of energy.

Answer: Animals cannot make organic substances (carbohydrates, fats and proteins) from inorganic substances, so they must take them in as food. Plants can make organic substances by photosynthesis: chlorophyll captures light energy from the Sun and transfers it into chemical energy stored in glucose and in the other substances made from it. Animals get this energy by eating plants, or by eating other animals that have eaten plants. So all the energy in an animal's food originally came from sunlight captured by plants — without plants, animals would have no supply of energy.

Question B5.02

Explain how chlorophyll helps photosynthesis to happen.

Answer: Chlorophyll is a green pigment found in chloroplasts. It captures (absorbs) energy from sunlight and immediately transfers this energy to water molecules and carbon dioxide molecules. This energy makes the carbon dioxide and water react together to produce glucose (and oxygen). In this way, chlorophyll transfers energy from light into chemical energy in carbohydrates. Without chlorophyll, light shining on carbon dioxide and water would never produce glucose.

Question B5.03

How many glucose molecules can be made from 12 molecules of carbon dioxide and 12 molecules of water?

Answer: 2 glucose molecules.

Working: the balanced equation is 6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂, so 6 molecules of carbon dioxide and 6 molecules of water make 1 molecule of glucose. 12 ÷ 6 = 2, so 12 molecules of carbon dioxide and 12 molecules of water make 2 molecules of glucose (and 12 molecules of oxygen). Check using carbon atoms: 12 CO₂ molecules contain 12 carbon atoms, and each glucose molecule needs 6, so 12 ÷ 6 = 2.

Question B5.04

Starch can easily be broken down to glucose by the enzyme amylase, which is found in plants. Cellulose is much more difficult to break down to glucose. Suggest how these differences between starch and cellulose relate to their functions in a plant.

Answer: Starch is the plant's energy store. When the plant needs energy (for example at night, or when a seed germinates), it must be able to turn the stored starch back into glucose quickly for respiration, so it is useful that amylase can break starch down easily. Cellulose forms the cell walls, which give each cell strength and support and must last for the whole life of the cell. If cellulose were easy to break down (for example by the plant's own amylase), the cell walls would be weakened or destroyed. Because cellulose is difficult to break down, the walls stay strong and permanent.

Question B5.05

Animals do not make or store starch. What is the substance that animals store, which is made of chains of glucose molecules?

Answer: Glycogen (stored mainly in the liver and muscles).

Question B5.06

Explain why some parts of a plant must have sucrose delivered to them.

Answer: Glucose can only be made by photosynthesis in the parts of a plant that contain chlorophyll, which usually means the leaves. Other parts, such as the roots, flowers, fruits, seeds, storage organs and the inner parts of the stem, have no chlorophyll (or get no light), so they cannot make their own glucose. Their cells still need carbohydrate: for respiration to release energy, to make cellulose for new cell walls as they grow, and to make starch for storage. Plants do not transport glucose — they change it into sucrose, which is carried in the phloem to these parts and changed back into glucose when it arrives.

Question B5.08

Making nectar costs a plant energy because it uses up glucose that the plant has made. Explain why the expense is worthwhile.

Answer: Nectar attracts pollinators — insects, bats or birds — to the flowers. The male gametes of a flower are inside pollen grains and cannot move themselves, so the plant relies on these animals to carry pollen from one flower to another. Animals will only do this if they get a reward, and nectar is that reward. Pollination allows fertilisation to happen, so the plant can reproduce sexually and produce seeds that grow into new plants. Without nectar, far fewer flowers would be pollinated, so the glucose used is a worthwhile price for successful reproduction.

Question B5.10

Explain how a palisade mesophyll cell is adapted for its function.

Answer: The function of a palisade mesophyll cell is photosynthesis. It is adapted in these ways:

  • It contains very large numbers of chloroplasts, which contain chlorophyll, so it can absorb as much light energy as possible.
  • It is tall and narrow, and the cells are packed tightly side by side, so a large number of cells (and chloroplasts) fit into the top part of the leaf, where the light is brightest.
  • It is close to the top of the leaf, just below the transparent cuticle and upper epidermis, so it receives plenty of sunlight.
  • It is close to the air spaces of the spongy mesophyll, which supply carbon dioxide by diffusion, and to the veins, which supply water.

Question B5.11

Suggest why the cells in the lower epidermis of a leaf (apart from the guard cells) do not contain chloroplasts.

Answer: Very little light reaches the lower epidermis. Sunlight falls mainly on the upper surface of the leaf, and most of it is absorbed by the chloroplasts in the palisade and spongy mesophyll before it reaches the bottom layer. Chloroplasts in the lower epidermis would therefore carry out very little photosynthesis, so making them would waste the plant's materials and energy. The job of the lower epidermis is to cover and protect the leaf and to contain the stomata for gas exchange, not to photosynthesise.

Revision and Exam Preparation

Exam Focus Priorities

B1 Cells and Organisms

  1. Learn MRS GREN with definitions — movement, respiration, sensitivity, growth, reproduction, excretion and nutrition. Questions often ask which feature is NOT a characteristic of all organisms: photosynthesis, breathing, a blood system and a nervous system are not.
  2. Respiration is not breathing — respiration is a set of chemical reactions in every living cell that releases energy from glucose; breathing is moving air in and out of the lungs, which plants and bacteria do not do.
  3. Growth must be permanent — define growth as a permanent increase in size and dry mass; the word permanent earns the mark.
  4. Know which structures are in which cells — animal and plant cells both have a cell membrane, cytoplasm, a nucleus, ribosomes and mitochondria; only plant cells have a cellulose cell wall, a large permanent vacuole with cell sap and (often) chloroplasts and starch grains.
  5. Bacteria have no nucleus — their DNA is a single circle in the cytoplasm, often with plasmids; their cell wall is not made of cellulose; they have no mitochondria or chloroplasts.
  6. Partially permeable versus fully permeable — the cell membrane is partially permeable and controls what enters and leaves the cell; the cell wall is fully permeable, supports the cell and stops it bursting, but does not control what enters.
  7. Mitochondria release energy — write that mitochondria release energy from glucose by aerobic respiration; never write that they make or produce energy.
  8. Ribosomes make proteins; DNA decides which proteins — the DNA in the nucleus carries the instructions, and the ribosomes link amino acids together to build the protein.
  9. No chloroplasts does not mean animal cell — many plant cells, such as root hair cells, have no chloroplasts; the cell wall is the reliable sign of a plant cell.
  10. Cell, tissue, organ, organ system, organism — a tissue is a group of similar cells; an organ is made of different tissues; be ready to give an example at each level (stomach lining cell, stomach lining tissue, stomach, digestive system, human).
  11. Show the magnification equation and substitution — write magnification = image size ÷ actual size, convert to the same units, substitute, then give the answer with a × sign and no units; each step can earn a mark.
  12. Divide to find the actual size — actual size = image size ÷ magnification; multiplying instead of dividing is the most common error in multiple-choice questions.
  13. Convert mm and µm correctly — multiply by 1000 to go from mm to µm, divide by 1000 to go from µm to mm, and check that your final size is sensible for the structure.
  14. Measure carefully and record the measurement — measure in mm with a ruler along the length asked for, and write the measurement in your working.
  15. Use the marks as a guide — a [6] question needs six separate creditworthy points, and a [2] definition usually needs two ideas.

B2 Movement into and out of Cells

  1. Learn the full definition of diffusion — include "net movement", "from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration", "down a concentration gradient" and "as a result of random movement"; each phrase can earn credit.
  2. Diffusion and osmosis are passive — their energy comes from the kinetic energy of the particles; never say they use energy from respiration, because only active transport does.
  3. Osmosis is only about water — it is the diffusion of water molecules through a partially permeable membrane; the solute does not move in osmosis.
  4. Use water potential correctly — a dilute solution has a high water potential, a concentrated solution has a low water potential, and water moves from high to low water potential.
  5. Explain why plant cells do not burst — the strong cell wall resists the swelling contents and presses back; use the words turgid and turgor pressure, and say animal cells burst because they have no cell wall.
  6. Do not mix up flaccid and plasmolysed — a flaccid cell has lost its turgor pressure and is soft; a plasmolysed cell has lost so much water that its cell membrane has torn away from the cell wall.
  7. The cell wall is fully permeable — it is the cell membrane, not the cell wall, that is partially permeable, so the space between the wall and the membrane of a plasmolysed cell fills with the outside solution.
  8. Active transport answers need three ideas — movement against a concentration gradient (low to high), carrier proteins in the membrane that change shape, and energy from respiration in mitochondria.
  9. Quote data from graphs and bar charts — give values with units and compare them (for example "potassium is 8 times more concentrated in the cells"), then use the data to decide which process is happening.
  10. Identify anomalous results properly — pick the point that is furthest from the line of best fit and does not fit the trend, and say why you chose it.
  11. Plan experiments using variables — name the independent, dependent and standardised variables, repeat readings and calculate a mean, and put units only in the column headings.
  12. Show working in surface area calculations — use cm² for area and cm³ for volume; cutting a cube into smaller cubes increases the total surface area but not the total volume.
  13. Common mistake: saying particles want to move — particles move randomly; the net movement happens because more particles move away from the high concentration than move into it.

B3 Biological Molecules

  1. Know the elements in each molecule — carbohydrates and fats contain only C, H and O; proteins contain C, H, O and N. Nitrogen is the element found in all proteins but not in carbohydrates or fats.
  2. Link each large molecule to its smaller building blocks — starch and glycogen are made from glucose; fats and oils from fatty acids and glycerol; proteins from amino acids. A common mistake is to say that fats are made of amino acids, or that starch is made of "sugar" without naming glucose.
  3. Starch is found in plants, glycogen in animals — glucose is found in both. Starch is the energy store in plant cells and glycogen is the energy store in animal cells.
  4. Only the Benedict's test needs heating — always write "heat in a water-bath (about 80 °C)". Never heat the biuret test; this is the error in Practice Question 4a(i).
  5. Always give the colour before and after — iodine orange-brown → blue-black; Benedict's blue → orange-red; biuret blue → violet; ethanol test → milky white emulsion. Write the colour you actually see (e.g. "stayed blue"), never just "no change" or "positive".
  6. Do not mix up the colours — iodine solution starts orange-brown, not blue. Benedict's and biuret both start blue, but Benedict's ends orange-red and biuret ends violet. Swapping the start and end colours, as in Practice Question 4a(ii), loses the mark.
  7. Describe the ethanol emulsion test in the right order — shake the food with ethanol first, then pour the ethanol (not the food) into water; a milky white emulsion means fat. Use very clean tubes.
  8. Read results tables carefully — "contains only fat" means positive for the ethanol emulsion test but negative for every other test (iodine stays brown, Benedict's stays blue, biuret stays blue).
  9. Benedict's tests for reducing sugars, not starch — the colour reached (green, yellow or orange-red) shows how much reducing sugar is present.
  10. Use the command words — identify (name, select or recognise), give (produce an answer from a given source or from recall), describe (state the points of a topic or give its main features). For "describe how you could test", give the reagent, what you do and the result.

B4 Enzymes

  1. Learn the definition of a catalyst exactly — "a substance that increases the rate of a chemical reaction and is not changed by the reaction". You need both parts: it speeds up the reaction and it is unchanged.
  2. Enzymes are proteins — they are made of amino acids and act as biological catalysts in all metabolic reactions. Without them, reactions would be too slow to sustain life.
  3. Use the word complementary — the active site has a shape that is complementary to the substrate. Do not say the active site is "the same shape" as the substrate.
  4. Name the enzyme–substrate complex — in Supplement answers, say that the substrate binds to the active site to form an enzyme–substrate complex, the products are released, and the enzyme is unchanged.
  5. Explain specificity using shape — only a substrate with a shape complementary to the active site can fit and bind, so each enzyme acts on only one type of substrate.
  6. Enzymes are denatured, not killed — enzymes are molecules, not living things, so never write that they "die" or are "killed".
  7. Low temperature does not denature enzymes — at low temperatures enzymes are slow because molecules have little kinetic energy; they work again when warmed. Denaturation happens above the optimum temperature or at a pH far from the optimum.
  8. Use the key words for temperature — kinetic energy, frequency of effective collisions, enzyme–substrate complex, shape of the active site, complementary, denatured.
  9. Explain pH effects in terms of shape — pH changes the shape of the enzyme and its active site. Kinetic energy and collisions are the explanation for temperature, not for pH.
  10. Describe graphs with numbers and units — quote the optimum (for example "about 37 °C") and the values where activity starts and stops. "Describe" means say what happens; "explain" means give the reasons.
  11. Time and rate are opposites — a shorter time means a faster rate (rate = 1 ÷ time). In the catalase, lipase and amylase experiments, the shortest time is at the optimum.
  12. Know the iodine test colours — blue-black means starch is present; brown means no starch. Do not say that iodine "turns clear".
  13. Spell enzyme names carefully — maltose (a sugar) and maltase (an enzyme) look almost the same; lipid is the substrate and lipase is the enzyme.
  14. Make controlled variables specific — write "temperature", "volume of amylase solution" or "concentration of starch solution", not just "the amount".
  15. Different enzymes have different optimums — most enzymes about pH 7 but the stomach protease about pH 2; human enzymes about 37 °C but some hot-spring bacteria up to 80 °C.

B5 Plant Nutrition

  1. Learn both photosynthesis equations exactly — Word equation: carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen. Balanced equation: 6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂. Write light and chlorophyll on the arrow, never as reactants, and check that the atoms balance.
  2. Chlorophyll transfers energy, it does not make it — Say that chlorophyll absorbs (traps) light energy and transfers it into chemical energy in glucose. Never write that plants "make energy".
  3. Know the starch test and the reason for every step — Boil in water (breaks cell membranes so iodine can reach the starch), turn off the flame, warm alcohol in a water bath (removes chlorophyll so the colour can be seen), soften in hot water, add iodine solution: blue-black means starch, orange-brown means none.
  4. Explain destarching — The plant is kept in the dark for about two days so it uses up its starch; any starch found later must have been made during the experiment.
  5. Always identify the control — Green parts of a variegated leaf (chlorophyll), the uncovered part of the leaf (light), the leaf in the flask of distilled water (carbon dioxide). The control is identical except for the factor being tested.
  6. Name the leaf tissues in order and link each to its function — Cuticle, upper epidermis, palisade mesophyll, spongy mesophyll with air spaces, vascular bundle (xylem and phloem), lower epidermis with stomata and guard cells.
  7. Know which cells contain chloroplasts — Palisade mesophyll cells have the most, spongy mesophyll cells have many, guard cells have a few; other epidermis cells and xylem have none. Use this to interpret data such as the bar chart in Practice Question 4.
  8. Get the routes of the raw materials right — Carbon dioxide diffuses in through the stomata and air spaces; water travels up the xylem and then moves into cells by osmosis. Carbon dioxide does not enter through the roots.
  9. Give a reason with each use of glucose — Starch (insoluble store, no effect on osmosis), sucrose (transport in phloem), cellulose (cell walls), amino acids and proteins (growth, need nitrate), chlorophyll (needs nitrate and magnesium), nectar (attracts pollinators), respiration (releases energy).
  10. Do not mix up nitrate and magnesium — Nitrate ions supply nitrogen for amino acids and proteins (deficiency: weak growth, yellow leaves); magnesium ions are needed for chlorophyll (deficiency: yellowing, especially between the veins).
  11. Plan rate experiments with clear variables — State the independent variable (light intensity via lamp distance, temperature, or carbon dioxide concentration), the dependent variable (bubbles or gas volume per minute), the controlled variables, the heat shield, and repeats with means.
  12. Draw graphs that earn every mark — Independent variable on the x-axis, both axes labelled with units, even scales filling at least half the grid, small neat crosses, ruled straight lines from point to point, nothing extended beyond the data.
  13. Describe, then explain, data — Describe the trend with numbers from the table or graph (for example "increased from 5 mm to 17 mm"), then explain it using raw materials, energy from light, enzymes or limiting factors.
  14. Avoid common mistakes — Photosynthesis does not "change carbon dioxide into oxygen" (the carbon goes into glucose); plants do not get their food from the soil (only water and mineral ions); bubble counts are not a perfect measure of oxygen because bubbles vary in size.

B1 Cells and Organisms

Chapter Summary

  • Seven characteristics: all organisms show seven characteristics: movement, respiration, sensitivity, growth, reproduction, excretion and nutrition.
  • Cells: all organisms are made of cells, and new cells are always made by the division of existing cells.
  • Animal and plant cells: both contain cytoplasm, a cell membrane, ribosomes, mitochondria and a nucleus containing DNA in the form of chromosomes. Plant cells also contain a cell wall, a large vacuole and sometimes chloroplasts.
  • Bacterial cells: they have cytoplasm, a cell membrane, a cell wall and ribosomes, but no nucleus. Their DNA is circular, and they may have extra, small circles of DNA called plasmids.
  • Membrane and wall: the cell membrane of all cells is partially permeable and controls what enters and leaves the cell. The cell wall is fully permeable and allows all molecules and ions to pass through it.
  • Ribosomes: the site of protein synthesis in a cell.
  • Mitochondria: release energy from glucose and other nutrients by aerobic respiration.
  • Chloroplasts: the site of photosynthesis.
  • Specialised cells: cells may be specialised for specific functions. Similar cells form tissues, tissues form organs, organs form organ systems, and organ systems make up the organism.
  • Magnification: magnification = size of image ÷ size of actual object.
  • Units: convert millimetres to micrometres by multiplying by 1000 (1 mm = 1000 µm).

B2 Movement into and out of Cells

Chapter Summary

  • Diffusion is the net movement of molecules or ions from a high concentration to a lower concentration, as a result of their random movement and kinetic energy.
  • Oxygen and carbon dioxide enter and leave cells by diffusion through the cell membranes, down a concentration gradient.
  • An increase in temperature, concentration gradient or surface area speeds up diffusion into a cell. This can be investigated using pieces of agar jelly.
  • Water diffuses through partially permeable membranes, including cell membranes, by osmosis.
  • A solution containing a lot of water has a high water potential; in osmosis, water diffuses down a water potential gradient.
  • Animal cells burst in pure water and shrink when placed in a concentrated solution. Plant cells do not burst in pure water because their cell wall stops this happening.
  • A plant cell in pure water takes up water by osmosis and becomes turgid, as its contents exert outward pressure on the cell wall. A plant cell in a concentrated solution loses water by osmosis. The cell becomes flaccid and may plasmolyse.
  • Active transport is the movement of particles across a cell membrane, up the concentration gradient, using energy from respiration.
  • Protein carriers in cell membranes change shape to move substances across the cell membrane during active transport.

B4 Enzymes

Chapter Summary

  • Enzymes are proteins that function as biological catalysts and increase the rate of metabolic reactions.
  • Without enzymes, these reactions would not take place fast enough to sustain life.
  • Each enzyme has an active site that has a complementary shape to its substrate.
  • When the substrate binds with the active site of the enzyme, the substrate is converted to product. The enzyme is unchanged.
  • The binding of substrate to the active site of the enzyme forms an enzyme–substrate complex.
  • Enzymes have an optimum temperature and pH at which they work most quickly. Higher temperatures or a very different pH cause the enzyme to change shape and denature.
  • As temperature increases, the kinetic energy of enzyme and substrate molecules increases, and they collide more frequently. This increases the rate of activity of the enzyme. However, above the optimum temperature, the enzyme begins to lose its shape, until it can no longer combine with the substrate. The shape of the active site is no longer complementary to the shape of the substrate.
